# 4.4 Slab Ocean

PlaSiC's slab ocean is a single mixed layer with a prescribed depth and finite heat capacity. It provides the atmosphere-facing sea surface temperature (SST). It has no prognostic current, salinity, stratification, or deep-ocean storage, and vertical and horizontal diffusion are disabled: each ocean point only exchanges heat with the atmosphere, the sea ice, and the deep ocean. The standard configuration also uses a three-dimensional ocean to supply a prognostic deep-ocean heat flux; it is documented in [4.5 Three-Dimensional Ocean](4.5-three-dimensional-ocean.md).

## 1. Heat budget

The only prognostic variable is the mixed-layer temperature $T_o$. With the default mixed-layer depth $H=50\ \mathrm{m}$, the heat capacity per unit area is

$$
 C_o=\rho_o c_{p,o}H=1030\times4180\times50\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}\approx2.15\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}.
$$

The slab is stepped once per sea-ice step, so $\Delta t$ is the step of [4.3 Thermodynamic Sea Ice](4.3-sea-ice-module.md). Two fluxes reach it, both positive into the ocean: the flux $Q_{i\to o}$ handed down by the ice module ([Figure 4.3.1, step 5](4.3-sea-ice-module.md)) and the deep-ocean flux $Q_{\rm deep}$. $Q_{\rm deep}$ is computed by the three-dimensional ocean and is zero in a slab-only build. The slab never receives the atmospheric flux $Q_a$ directly: every sea point, ice-covered or not, passes through the ice module first, and what reaches the slab from the atmosphere is contained in $Q_{i\to o}$.

Over open water ($h_i=0$ after the ice update), the two fluxes change the temperature directly:

$$
 T_o^{*}=T_o^n+\frac{\Delta t}{C_o}\left(Q_{i\to o}+Q_{\rm deep}\right).
$$

Over open water above the freezing point, $Q_{i\to o}$ is $Q_a$ less the latent heat of any snow that falls on open water ([Figure 4.3.1, step 4a](4.3-sea-ice-module.md)). Where the ice has just melted completely, it also carries the melting energy left over (Section 2).

Under ice ($h_i>0$), the temperature does not respond to these fluxes, so $T_o^*=T_o^n$. The ice module hands down only $Q_{i\to o}=Q_f=K_z(T_f-T_o)$, the segment of the ice-column conductive flux between $T_f$ and $T_o$ ([Figure 4.3.1, steps 3 and 5](4.3-sea-ice-module.md)), and the slab passes it on to the ice together with $Q_{\rm deep}$.

Wherever there is ice, and where open water started the step below the freezing point $T_f=271.25\ \mathrm{K}$, the slab then converts the departure from $T_f$ into latent heat of ice and sets $T_o^{n+1}=T_f$. The flux returned to the ice is

$$
 Q_{o\to i}=
 \begin{cases}
 Q_{i\to o}+Q_{\rm deep}+\dfrac{C_o}{\Delta t}\left(T_o^{*}-T_f\right), & \text{under ice},\\[8pt]
 \dfrac{C_o}{\Delta t}\left(T_o^{*}-T_f\right), & \text{open water with } T_o^n<T_f,\\[8pt]
 0, & \text{open water with } T_o^n\ge T_f,
 \end{cases}
$$

where a positive $Q_{o\to i}$ melts ice and a negative one forms ice. Under ice the correction always applies, so the slab stays at $T_f$. The next ice step then starts from $T_o=T_f$, where $Q_f=0$; under established ice the ice module hands down nothing and $Q_{o\to i}=Q_{\rm deep}$. A point that cools below $T_f$ during one step therefore keeps that SST until the next step converts the deficit into ice. On open water with $T_o^n\ge T_f$, the slab simply takes $T_o^{n+1}=T_o^*$.

Without a prognostic ocean the SST is set to climatology. With `ocean.flux_correction = 1` (off by default), the correction under ice is replaced by a restoring flux toward the climatological SST. Figure 4.4.1 shows these paths.

## 2. Coupling with sea ice

The slab runs inside the sea-ice step, after the ice has been updated. At a sea-ice coupling time, the order is:

1. the atmosphere hands the ice module the heat flux $Q_a$, averaged over the coupling window, together with snowfall, freshwater and wind stress;
2. the ice module computes the surface temperature, snow melt, and conductive flux, using the $T_o$ of the previous step;
3. the ice module updates the thickness with the $Q_{o\to i}$ that the slab returned at the previous step;
4. the ice module hands $Q_{i\to o}$ to the slab; the deep ocean and the slab then step forward, apply the heat budget of Section 1, enforce $T_f$, and return a new $Q_{o\to i}$ that the ice consumes at the next step;
5. the sea interface sends $T_i$, the new $T_o$, cover, thickness, and snow back to the atmosphere.

The freezing-point correction is therefore an energy transfer, not an arbitrary temperature limiter. Because the slab runs after the ice, the ice receives $Q_{o\to i}$ one step late; [Figure 4.3.1, step 5](4.3-sea-ice-module.md) shows where this lag enters the ice ledger. Energy left over after the ice has melted completely moves the other way within the same step: the ice kernel adds it to $Q_{i\to o}$ before handing it down (item 4 above), and the slab can warm in the same step.
