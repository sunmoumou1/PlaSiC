# 2.3 Spherical Harmonic Spectra & Transforms Method

PlaSiC uses a spherical harmonic spectral method in the horizontal. State variables such as temperature, vorticity, divergence, and the logarithm of surface pressure are stored mainly as spherical harmonic coefficients, whereas nonlinear terms and physical parameterizations are computed on grid. The model therefore moves back and forth between spectral and grid space several times per time step (the spectral transform method).

Thus:

- The **spectral method** answers "which basis functions represent the field";
- the **spectral transform method** answers "how to move between spectral and grid space and how to distribute the work between them".

PlaSiC delegates the transforms between spherical harmonics and the latitude–longitude grid to the mature [SHTns](https://nschaeff.bitbucket.io/shtns/) library (Schaeffer, 2013); these generic numerical algorithms are no longer expanded inside the project. This removes code that is not central to a GCM.

## 1. Why several horizontal representations?

Reading PlaSiC you will meet three horizontal representations.

| Representation | Typical symbol | Meaning | Operations suited to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grid space | $Q_{k,j}$ | field values at each latitude–longitude grid point | nonlinear products, local physical parameterizations |
| Fourier space | $F^m(\mu_j)$ | coefficients of each wavenumber on a latitude circle | bridge between the longitude FFT and the latitude associate Legendre transform |
| Spherical harmonic space | $Q_n^m$ | coefficients of each spherical harmonic mode on the sphere | horizontal derivatives, the Laplacian, spectral dissipation |

Here $\lambda$ is longitude, $\varphi$ geographic latitude, $\mu=\sin\varphi$, $m$ the zonal wavenumber, and $n$ the total wavenumber of a spherical harmonic. The mathematical relation among the three representations is

```text
spherical harmonic space          Fourier space                grid space
   Q_n^m  <-- associated Legendre -->  F^m(mu_j)  <-- FFT -->  Q(lambda_k, mu_j)
```


## 2. Spectral method

The spectral method represents a spatial field with a set of global basis functions (Eliasen et al., 1970; Orszag, 1970; Bourke, 1972; Machenhauer, 1979).

$$
Q(\lambda,\mu,\sigma,t)
=
\sum_{m=-M}^{M}\sum_{n=|m|}^{N}
Q_n^m(\sigma,t)\,
\widetilde P_n^m(\mu)e^{im\lambda}.
$$

At this stage $\widetilde P_n^m$ is intentionally a **generic notation**: it means an associated Legendre function with whatever normalization and phase convention is being used.

Many linear differential operations become simple multiplications in this basis. For example,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}e^{im\lambda}
=im\,e^{im\lambda},
$$

and the spherical Laplacian acting on $Y_n^m$ merely multiplies it by an eigenvalue that depends on $n$. Horizontal derivatives, spectral dissipation, and some linear dynamics are therefore best computed in spectral space.

## 3. Why a spherical harmonic splits into two factors

A spherical harmonic can be read as the product of a longitude Fourier wave and a latitude associate Legendre structure:

$$
Y_n^m(\lambda,\mu)
=\widetilde P_n^m(\mu)e^{im\lambda}.
$$

This equation is schematic until the normalization is fixed. For the PlaSiC latitude-normalized convention, substitute $\widetilde P_n^m=\overline P_n^m$. SHTns later uses a globally orthonormal version with an additional factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$; that is a normalization of the complete $Y_n^m$, not a redefinition of $\overline P_n^m$.

![Real spherical harmonic modes, shown over the two longitude halves of the sphere](<../assets/images/spherical-harmonic-modes.png>)

*Figure 2.3.1 | Real spherical harmonic modes arranged by total wavenumber $n$ (rows) and zonal wavenumber $m=-n,\ldots,n$ (position within a row). Each tile is a latitude–longitude map of one mode. The two panels are stacked vertically: panel A unwraps $0\leq\lambda\leq\pi$ and panel B unwraps $\pi\leq\lambda\leq2\pi$, so together they show the complete sphere and mark the longitude range explicitly. In the PlaSiC real convention, $Y_n^m=\overline{P}_n^{|m|}(\mu)\cos(m\lambda)$ for $m>0$ and $Y_n^m=\overline{P}_n^{|m|}(\mu)\sin(|m|\lambda)$ for $m<0$; the latitude factor sets the meridional structure and the Fourier factor sets the number of longitudinal waves. Colours use one shared scale for the basis values (blue negative, red positive). Tile labels report the signed $Q_{n,\mathrm{real}}^m=\operatorname{Re}Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m$ for cosine modes, the signed $Q_{n,\mathrm{img}}^m=\operatorname{Im}Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m$ for sine modes, and the signed $Q_n^0$ for the zonal mode. The lower map shows the illustrative two-lobe field used to compute these coefficients, synthesized with modes through $n=8$.*

### 3.1 Longitude: Fourier modes

Holding latitude, height, and time fixed, $Q$ can be written as a superposition of periodic functions of longitude:

$$
Q(\lambda,\mu)=\sum_m F^m(\mu)e^{im\lambda}.
$$

- $m=0$ is the zonal mean, constant along a latitude circle;
- $m=1$ completes one wave around the globe;
- larger $m$ generally means smaller longitudinal scale.

Longitude grid points are equally spaced and periodic, so the discrete Fourier transform can be computed efficiently with the fast Fourier transform (Cooley & Tukey, 1965). The FFT is a fast algorithm for the same discrete transform: direct summation needs about $O(N_\lambda^2)$ operations, whereas the FFT typically needs about $O(N_\lambda\log N_\lambda)$.

### 3.2 Latitude: Legendre functions

Ordinary Legendre polynomials are defined by Rodrigues' formula:

$$
P_n(\mu)=\frac{1}{2^n n!}
\frac{d^n}{d\mu^n}(\mu^2-1)^n.
$$

For example,

$$
P_0(\mu)=1,\qquad
P_1(\mu)=\mu,\qquad
P_2(\mu)=\frac12(3\mu^2-1).
$$

![Legendre polynomials of degree 0 to 5](<../assets/images/legendre-polynomials.svg>)

*Figure 2.3.2 | $P_0$ through $P_5$. As the degree $n$ increases, the number of zeros in $(-1,1)$ and the meridional structure grow; every ordinary Legendre polynomial equals 1 at $\mu=1$.*

Ordinary Legendre polynomials correspond to $m=0$ and describe axisymmetric meridional structure. A field that varies in both longitude and latitude needs associated Legendre functions. A common definition is

$$
P_n^m(\mu)=(-1)^m(1-\mu^2)^{m/2}
\frac{d^m}{d\mu^m}P_n(\mu),
\qquad 0\le m\le n.
$$

To compare shapes for different $m$ in one figure, Figure 2.3.3 uses the unit-norm normalization adopted by PlaSiC:

$$
\overline P_n^m(\mu)
=
\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}\frac{(n-m)!}{(n+m)!}}\,
P_n^m(\mu),
\qquad
\int_{-1}^{1}\left[\overline P_n^m(\mu)\right]^2d\mu=1.
$$

Thus, for the convention used by PlaSiC,

$$
\boxed{\widetilde P_n^m(\mu)\equiv\overline P_n^m(\mu)}
$$

once the generic notation from Section 2.1 has been made explicit. More generally, another textbook or library may write

$$
\widetilde P_n^m(\mu)=C_{nm}P_n^m(\mu),
$$

with a different coefficient $C_{nm}$. Such a choice changes the numerical spectral coefficients inversely, but not the represented physical field. The formula above is the specific $C_{nm}$ used by PlaSiC.

![Associated Legendre functions of total wavenumber 5 and different zonal wavenumbers](<../assets/images/associated-legendre-functions.svg>)

*Figure 2.3.3 | Normalized associated Legendre functions at $n=5$ for different $m$. For $m>0$ the factor $(1-\mu^2)^{m/2}$ forces the function to vanish at the poles $\mu=\pm1$; larger $m$ concentrates the structure at low latitudes. The curves are normalized to unit norm, so the vertical axis cannot be used to compare the raw amplitudes of unnormalized functions.*


### 3.3 Triangular truncation

PlaSiC uses triangular truncation; for T21, it retains all modes with

$$
0\le n\le21,\qquad |m|\le n.
$$

For a real field, negative-$m$ coefficients follow from positive-$m$ coefficients by conjugation, so the code stores only the $m\ge0$ half explicitly.

## 4. Spherical harmonic transform

This section keeps continuous formulas separate from the discrete formulas used in the program. We now fix the notation completely: all latitude projections and reconstructions below use the PlaSiC unit-norm functions $\overline P_n^m$ defined in Section 3.2. Thus the generic $\widetilde P_n^m$ is replaced by $\overline P_n^m$ here; it should not be interpreted as an additional normalization.

$$
\int_{-1}^{1}
\overline P_n^m(\mu)\overline P_{n'}^m(\mu)\,d\mu
=\delta_{nn'}.
$$

PlaSiC handles real fields and stores only the complex coefficients with $m\ge0$. Under its historical normalization a scalar field is written as

$$
Q(\lambda,\mu)
=\sum_{n=0}^{N}
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^0\overline P_n^0(\mu)
+2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=m}^{N}
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m
\overline P_n^m(\mu)e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda}
\right].
$$

$$
F^m(\mu)
=\frac{1}{2\pi}
\int_0^{2\pi}
Q(\lambda,\mu)e^{-\mathrm{i}m\lambda}\,d\lambda
=
\sum_{n=m}^{N}
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^{m}
\overline P_n^m(\mu).
$$


### 4.1 From spectral to grid space: synthesis

Synthesis first sums over the total wavenumber $n$ at every Gaussian latitude $\mu_j$:

$$
F_j^m
\equiv F^m(\mu_j)
=\sum_{n=|m|}^{N}
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m
\overline P_n^m(\mu_j).
$$

Now $F_j^m$ is the $m$-th longitude Fourier coefficient on the $j$-th latitude circle. For real fields $F_j^{-m}=(F_j^m)^*$, so the inverse discrete Fourier transform subsequently performed on each latitude circle can be written as

$$
Q_{k,j}
=F_j^0
+2\operatorname{Re}
\left[
\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_j^m e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda_k}
\right].
$$

The program therefore stores only the $m\ge0$ complex coefficients. Internally SHTns calls FFTW (Frigo & Johnson, 2005) for the longitude sums and returns the real grid field directly. The two steps together are called spherical harmonic **synthesis**.

### 4.2 From grid to spectral space: analysis

The return direction first computes discrete Fourier coefficients on every Gaussian latitude circle:

$$
F_j^m
=\frac{1}{N_\lambda}
\sum_{k=0}^{N_\lambda-1}
Q_{k,j}e^{-\mathrm{i}m\lambda_k}
=\frac{1}{N_\lambda}
\sum_{k=0}^{N_\lambda-1}
Q_{k,j}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}mk/N_\lambda}.
$$

The factor $1/N_\lambda$ matches the $1/(2\pi)$ of the continuous definition exactly, because an equally spaced longitude grid has step $\Delta\lambda=2\pi/N_\lambda$:

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}(\cdots)d\lambda
\;\longrightarrow\;
\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2\pi}\sum_k(\cdots)
=\frac{1}{N_\lambda}\sum_k(\cdots).
$$

As long as $N_\lambda>2M$, every retained zonal wavenumber lies below the grid's Nyquist wavenumber $N_\lambda/2$, so the discrete Fourier analysis returns these coefficients exactly.

Once $F^m(\mu)$ is known, the latitude orthonormality of $\overline P_n^m$ gives

$$
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m
=\int_{-1}^{1}
F^m(\mu)\overline P_n^m(\mu)\,d\mu.
$$

On $J$ Gaussian latitudes the discrete form is

$$
\boxed{
Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m
=\sum_{j=1}^{J}
w_jF_j^m\overline P_n^m(\mu_j)
}
$$

Nor is a complex conjugate needed on $\overline P_n^m$, because it is real for $\mu\in[-1,1]$; the conjugation required in longitude has already been performed by $e^{-\mathrm{i}m\lambda}$.

However, SHTns uses the globally orthonormal basis

$$
Y_n^m
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
\overline P_n^m(\mu)e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda},
$$

The factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ belongs to the **complete spherical-harmonic basis** and is not part of the latitude function $\overline P_n^m$. Equivalently, PlaSiC uses the latitude-normalized factor $\overline P_n^m e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda}$ in its expansion, whereas SHTns uses the globally normalized factor $Y_n^m$.

Its analysis coefficients therefore satisfy

$$
\boxed{
Q_{n,\mathrm{SHTns}}^m
=\sqrt{2\pi}\,Q_{n,\mathrm{PlaSiC}}^m
}.
$$

## 5. Gaussian grids, quadrature, and aliasing

### 5.1 Where Gaussian latitudes come from

For the moment set the spherical harmonic transform aside and consider a one-dimensional integral:

$$
I[f]=\int_{-1}^{1}f(\mu)\,d\mu.
$$

Numerical integration wants to replace "the area under the whole curve" by a weighted sum of finitely many function values:

$$
I[f]\approx \sum_{j=1}^{J}w_j f(\mu_j).
$$

Two kinds of quantities must be determined:

- the **nodes** $\mu_j$ answer "where to sample";
- the **weights** $w_j$ answer "how much each sample contributes".

They are quadrature coefficients chosen so that the weighted sum is as accurate as possible (for the standard properties of Gaussian quadrature see Abramowitz & Stegun, 1964; Haltiner & Williams, 1982).

Their relation to the spherical grid is as follows: the $J=N_\varphi$ nodes $\mu_j$ are the zeros of $P_J$, and the geographic latitudes follow from

$$
\varphi_j=\arcsin\mu_j.
$$

Gaussian nodes exclude the poles, because all zeros of $P_J$ lie in the open interval $(-1,1)$.

Two distinct uses must be kept apart:

- the ordinary Legendre polynomial $P_{N_\varphi}$ determines the positions of the Gaussian latitudes;
- the associated Legendre functions $P_n^m$ for different $m$ represent general spherical harmonic modes.

Gaussian points are not equally spaced in $\mu$, nor exactly equally spaced in geographic latitude. They are chosen first of all to perform the spectral projection exactly with as few latitude circles as possible, not to make the map's latitude spacing uniform. The contribution of each latitude circle to the integral is likewise not a plain average over grid points: it must carry the corresponding weight $w_j$.

![T42 Gaussian grid versus regular latitude–longitude grid](<../assets/images/T42_gaussian_vs_regular_panels_ab.png>)

*Figure 2.3.4 | T42 grids with $64\times128$ points. (a) Gaussian latitudes (blue) and a regular latitude grid (grey dashed), each with 64 latitude circles. The spacing between adjacent Gaussian circles is slightly compressed near the poles and is largest around the equator; the inset shows this weak variation. (b) Difference $\varphi_G-\varphi_R$ between corresponding Gaussian and regular latitudes. The offset is antisymmetric about the equator and reaches $0.73^\circ$, so it is small (less than $1^\circ$) but relevant when reconstructing or diagnosing fields on a regular grid.*

How these nodes and weights are computed bit by bit is described in the appendix [The complete mathematics behind gaussmod_kernels](../9-appendix/9.3-mathematical-foundations-of-gaussmod_kernels.md).

### 5.2 Why the grid is finer than the spectral truncation

In a spectral model, variables are represented by a finite spherical harmonic expansion. Atmospheric models also frequently need nonlinear terms, such as the advection term

$$
\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla T,
$$

or the product of two variables:

$$
C(\lambda,\mu)=A(\lambda,\mu)B(\lambda,\mu).
$$

Because multiplication in spectral space is convolution, the product of two band-limited fields contains higher wavenumbers.

Suppose both fields are truncated at wavenumber $M$:

$$
A\sim e^{\mathrm{i}m_1\lambda},\quad
B\sim e^{\mathrm{i}m_2\lambda},
$$

with

$$
|m_1|,|m_2|\leq M.
$$

Their product is then

$$
AB\sim e^{\mathrm{i}(m_1+m_2)\lambda}.
$$

The product wavenumber is the sum of the two input wavenumbers, so

$$
|m_1+m_2|\leq 2M.
$$

The largest absolute wavenumber, reached when the two input modes have the same sign, is therefore $2M$. That is,

$$
\boxed{\text{the product of two fields truncated at }M\text{ contains wavenumbers }|m|\leq2M}
$$

This is why nonlinear computations generate wavenumbers above the original spectral truncation.


A discrete grid, however, can represent only a limited range of wavenumbers. At the longitude grid points

$$
\lambda_k=\frac{2\pi k}{N_\lambda},
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,N_\lambda-1,
$$

the following identity holds for every integer $m$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{\mathrm{i}(m-N_\lambda)\lambda_k}
&=e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda_k}e^{-\mathrm{i}N_\lambda\lambda_k}\\
&=e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda_k}e^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi k}\\
&=e^{\mathrm{i}m\lambda_k},
\end{aligned}
$$

because $e^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi k}=1$ for integer $k$. The equality is pointwise on the sampled grid; as continuous functions of an arbitrary longitude $\lambda$, the two waves are different. More generally, all wavenumbers $m+\ell N_\lambda$ with integer $\ell$ produce the same sampled values. The discrete transform therefore sees wavenumbers modulo $N_\lambda$.

If a generated mode has wavenumber $p>N_\lambda/2$, choose an integer $\ell$ so that

$$
p_{\mathrm{alias}}=p-\ell N_\lambda
$$

lies in the signed Nyquist interval. In the first-wrap case $N_\lambda/2<p\leq N_\lambda$, this is $p-N_\lambda$, which lies on the negative-wavenumber side. Thus a product mode with $p=2M$ is folded to $2M-N_\lambda$ in that case. On a grid that resolves only up to $M$ (that is, $N_\lambda=2M$), the $2M$ mode folds exactly onto wavenumber zero:

$$
2M \rightarrow 0.
$$

The one-dimensional example below makes this fold visible. Take $M=3$ and
$A=B=\cos(3\lambda)$, so that
$AB=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos(6\lambda)$. With the minimum $N_\lambda=6$
grid, every sampled product is equal to $1$ and the discrete transform reports
the $k=6$ contribution as a spurious mean ($k=0$). With the $N_\lambda=10$
grid required by the $3/2$ rule, the same contribution is folded to $k=-4$,
which lies outside the retained band $|k|\le M$ and can be discarded.

![One-dimensional illustration of spectral aliasing and the 3/2 grid rule](<../assets/images/aliasing_demo_3over2.png>)

*Figure 2.3.5 | Aliasing in the quadratic product $C=A B$ for $A=B=\cos(3\lambda)$. Orange curves show the continuous product, blue dots are the longitude samples, and teal dashed curves show the reconstruction from the retained band $|k|\le3$. (a,c) The minimum grid $N_\lambda=2M=6$ folds the product mode $k=6$ onto $k=0$, turning the sampled product into a constant. (b,d) The $3/2$ grid $N_\lambda=3M+1=10$ folds $k=6$ to $k=-4$, outside the retained band, so the low-wavenumber reconstruction keeps the correct mean. Open bars mark the exact continuous coefficients; filled bars are the coefficients obtained from the sampled grid.*

This folding of high wavenumbers onto low ones is called

$$
\boxed{\text{aliasing}}
$$

Aliasing has several consequences:

1. high-wavenumber errors contaminate low-wavenumber spectral coefficients;
2. the large-scale circulation is distorted;
3. energy transfer between scales is misrepresented;
4. the stability of the time integration is degraded.

To keep aliasing out of the retained band, the transform grid must be finer than the spectral truncation. The product can reach the positive wavenumber $p=2M$, whose first alias is $2M-N_\lambda$. For this alias, and by symmetry the alias from $p=-2M$, to lie outside the retained band $|m|\le M$, require

$$
2M-N_\lambda<-M,
$$

that is

$$
N_\lambda>3M,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
N_\lambda\ge3M+1.
$$

This is the **3/2 dealiasing rule** (Orszag, 1971): the transform grid needs roughly one and a half times the spectral truncation,

$$
M_{\mathrm{grid}}\approx\frac{3}{2}M.
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{the transform grid resolution is about }1.5\text{ times the spectral truncation}
}
$$

The grid thus carries the extra wavenumbers created by the nonlinear products; after the analysis, all modes above the truncation are simply discarded. Note that resolving the product spectrum itself (up to $2M$) without any aliasing would require $N_\lambda\ge4M+1$; the 3/2 rule is sufficient because the aliases only have to miss the retained band, not vanish.

### 5.3 Grid settings in PlaSiC

The truncation is chosen to satisfy

$$
N_{\mathrm{TRU}}
=
\left\lfloor\frac{N_{\lambda}-1}{3}\right\rfloor,
$$

where

- $N_{\mathrm{TRU}}$ is the maximum wavenumber of the triangular truncation;
- $N_{\lambda}$ controls the highest wavenumber the longitude direction can resolve.

For example, with

$$
N_{\lambda}=128,
\qquad
N_{\varphi}=64,
$$

one gets

$$
N_{\mathrm{TRU}}
=
\frac{128-1}{3}
=42
$$

(integer division), so the grid corresponds to a

$$
T42
$$

spectral truncation. Concretely:

- the model finally retains only spherical harmonic coefficients with $n\leq42$;
- for nonlinear computations it first transforms the spectral fields to the finer $128\times64$ grid;
- products and other nonlinear operations are evaluated on that grid;
- the result is transformed back to spectral space;
- modes above T42 are truncated away.

## 6. Adaptation conventions between PlaSiC and SHTns

### 6.1 Layout of spectral coefficients

PlaSiC stores the triangular-truncated spectrum with $m$ as the outer index and $n$ as the inner index:

```text
(m=0,n=0), (m=0,n=1), ...,
(m=1,n=1), (m=1,n=2), ...
```

Each complex coefficient is stored as an interleaved single-precision array:

```text
[real(0), imag(0), real(1), imag(1), ...]
```

The SHTns interface uses complex arrays. The adaptation layer copies and converts types, and always sets the imaginary part of $m=0$ modes to zero.

### 6.2 Wind fields and the Robert form

PlaSiC uses the Robert form for the horizontal wind (Robert, 1966):

$$
U=u\cos\varphi,\qquad V=v\cos\varphi,
$$

where $u$ is the eastward wind and $v$ the northward wind. The SHTns configuration is created with `SHT_ROBERT_FORM`. The adaptation layer also handles the sign difference between the southward $\theta$ component of SHTns and the northward component of PlaSiC.

The complete mathematics of the vector spherical harmonic transform is given in the appendix [The complete mathematics behind spat_to_SHsphtor](../9-appendix/9.2-mathematical-foundations-of-spat_to_shsphtor.md).

### 6.3 High-level interface

| Routine | Mathematical role |
|---|---|
| `sht_scalar_to_grid` | scalar spherical harmonic spectrum $\rightarrow$ grid field |
| `sht_grid_to_scalar` | grid field $\rightarrow$ scalar spherical harmonic spectrum |
| `sht_vortdiv_to_wind` | divergence/vorticity spectrum $\rightarrow$ eastward/northward wind |
| `sht_wind_to_vortdiv` | eastward/northward wind $\rightarrow$ divergence/vorticity spectrum |
| `sht_scalar_to_grid_gradient` | scalar spectrum $\rightarrow$ Robert-form northward/eastward gradient |
| `sht_qtend` | scalar source terms and horizontal fluxes $\rightarrow$ scalar spectral tendencies |
| `sht_mktend` | momentum- and temperature-related grid terms $\rightarrow$ divergence, temperature, and vorticity spectral tendencies |

Dynamical code then only has to say which physical field goes to which space; it no longer calls basic mathematical routines explicitly.
