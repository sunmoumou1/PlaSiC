# 2.6 Leapfrog Time Stepping

This note continues the time-level notation of the dynamical-core entry note
([2.1 Governing Equations](<2.1-governing-equations.md>); the discrete form of that notation is
collected in [2.2 Ten-Step Algorithm](<2.2-ten-step-algorithm.md>)). It explains how the adiabatic
tendencies form the candidate new level and why the leapfrog scheme produces a computational mode;
the companion note [2.7 Robert–Asselin Filtering](<2.7-robert-asselin-filtering.md>) explains how
that mode is damped.

## Leapfrog update { #leapfrog-update }

For a tendency \(R^j\), the standard leapfrog scheme is

$$
X^{j+1}=X^{j-1}+2\Delta\tau R^j.
\tag{2.6.1}
$$

It is a centered scheme that is second-order accurate in time and free of numerical dissipation
(Haltiner & Williams, 1982; Durran, 2010), and it needs the tendency only at the current time
level. In spectral models, this scheme and its time filter are usually associated with the work of
Robert (1966) and Asselin (1972).

## Computational mode of the leapfrog scheme { #computational-mode }

An "oscillation that flips sign from one time level to the next" does not mean that temperature,
pressure, or wind speed themselves must change sign at every time step. It means that the numerical
solution may contain an error component of the form \(a(-1)^j\): this component is \(+a\) at step
\(j\) and \(-a\) at step \(j+1\), so it flips sign at every step.

This extra solution comes from the two-step recursion of the leapfrog scheme (classical analyses
of the computational mode are given by Robert, 1966, and Asselin, 1972). Consider the simplest
equation

$$
\frac{dX}{d\tau}=0.
$$

Its physical solution is clearly the constant \(X(\tau)=X_0\). For this equation, however, the
leapfrog scheme (2.6.1) gives

$$
X^{j+1}=X^{j-1}.
$$

All even time levels therefore copy \(X^0\), and all odd time levels copy \(X^1\):

```text
even chain: X⁰ ─────→ X² ─────→ X⁴ ─────→ ...
odd chain:      X¹ ─────→ X³ ─────→ X⁵ ─────→ ...
```

If \(X^0\) and \(X^1\) are not exactly equal at start-up, the two time subsequences never coincide.
Define

$$
\overline X=\frac{X^0+X^1}{2},
\qquad
a=\frac{X^0-X^1}{2},
$$

so that every time level can be written in the single form

$$
X^j=\underbrace{\overline X}_{\text{physical solution}}
+\underbrace{a(-1)^j}_{\text{computational mode}}.
$$

For example, with \(X^0=10.1\) and \(X^1=9.9\), the leapfrog scheme produces

```text
10.1, 9.9, 10.1, 9.9, 10.1, 9.9, ...
```

This is the constant physical solution \(10.0\) plus the computational mode
\(+0.1,-0.1,+0.1,-0.1,\ldots\). The quantity that alternates in sign is this \(0.1\) error, not
the total variable \(X\).

Why does the leapfrog equation not detect this obvious oscillation? Substitute the computational
mode into the centered time difference:

$$
\frac{a(-1)^{j+1}-a(-1)^{j-1}}{2\Delta\tau}=0.
$$

Since \((-1)^{j+1}=(-1)^{j-1}\), the centered difference compares values two time steps apart, and
the computational mode has the same sign two steps apart. To the leapfrog operator, this fast
oscillation therefore looks exactly like "the time derivative is zero". This is the precise
meaning of the statement that the leapfrog scheme admits a computational mode in addition to the
physical solution.

Even if the odd and even time levels are made identical at the start, rounding error, nonlinear
interactions, and physical parameterizations can continually excite a difference between the two
time chains. The standard leapfrog scheme has no numerical dissipation, so it cannot damp this
error by itself; a long integration therefore needs the dedicated time filter of
[2.7 Robert–Asselin Filtering](<2.7-robert-asselin-filtering.md>).
