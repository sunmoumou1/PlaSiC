# 2.5 Semi-Implicit Gravity-Wave Solver { #_1 }

This note is built around one question: how does PlaSiC retain the dynamical effect of
gravity waves while removing the severe restriction that fast gravity waves impose on the
explicit time step?

The conclusion comes first. The algorithm neither filters out gravity waves nor makes every
dynamical term implicit. It extracts only the linear coupling among divergence, temperature,
and column mass around a fixed reference temperature profile \(T_0(\sigma)\), and solves that
coupling implicitly at the midpoint of the leapfrog interval (Kwizak & Robert, 1971;
Hoskins & Simmons, 1975; Simmons et al., 1978). The grid-point nonlinear dynamics are still
evaluated explicitly at the current time level.


The following symbols are used throughout this note:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(L_p=\Delta\boldsymbol\sigma^{\mathsf T}\) | the \(1\times L\) row vector that performs the σ-column integral of the layer divergences; the subscript \(p\) indicates that it acts in the surface pressure equation |
| \(\mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}\), \(\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}\) | divergence and temperature tendencies evaluated explicitly at the current time level |
| \(\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI}\) | time-centered divergence obtained by the semi-implicit solve |
| \(\Delta\tau\) | spacing between adjacent time levels; a complete leapfrog interval is \(2\Delta\tau\) |
| \(L\) | number of full vertical levels |

## 1. Why mode splitting is needed

### 1.1 The restriction of a fully explicit time step

The divergence, temperature, and surface pressure are linked by a fast gravity-wave coupling.
If the whole system were advanced explicitly, the stable time step would have to satisfy a very
restrictive Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition (Courant et al., 1928; Durran, 2010):

$$
\Delta t\lesssim\frac{\Delta x}{c_g},
$$

where \(c_g\) is the gravity-wave phase speed. For a global low-resolution climate integration,
weather-scale advection does not require such a small time step, so a fully explicit scheme
would waste much of the computation on resolving fast waves that are not the focus of the study.

For a concrete estimate, take the reference profile used by the solver, \(T_0=250\ \mathrm{K}\).
The fastest wave of the linearized system is,

$$
c_g=\sqrt{\frac{c_p}{c_v} R_dT_0}\approx317\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$

Using the equatorial spacing of the Gaussian grid, \(\Delta x=\pi a/N_{\mathrm{lat}}\), the fully explicit limit
\(\Delta t_{\max}=\Delta x/c_g\) is:

| Truncation | Gaussian grid | \(\Delta x\) (km) | Fully explicit CFL limit (min) | PlaSiC step (min) | PlaSiC / explicit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T21 | \(64\times32\) | 625 | 32.9 | 45 | 1.4 |
| T31 | \(96\times48\) | 417 | 21.9 | 36 | 1.6 |
| T42 | \(128\times64\) | 313 | 16.4 | 30 | 1.8 |
| T85 | \(256\times128\) | 156 | 8.2 | 15 | 1.8 |


### 1.2 Splitting the linear and nonlinear parts

The linear reference state is an atmosphere at rest with horizontally uniform surface
pressure and a temperature profile \(T_0(\sigma)\). Write \(\pi=\ln(p_s/p_0)\) and
\(T=T_0+T'\). With \(G\) the vertical hydrostatic integration matrix, the full
geopotential at each \(\sigma\) level is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi&=\Phi_s+GT_0+\Phi_{\rm lin}+\delta\Phi,\\
\delta\Phi&=G(T_v-T),\\
T_v-T&=\left(\frac{R_v}{R_d}-1\right)qT.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reference contribution \(GT_0\) is horizontally uniform on each layer, so
\(\nabla_h^2(GT_0)=0\). The prescribed surface geopotential \(\Phi_s\) supplies a fixed
forcing. The fast linear equations take the form shown in
[Step 2 of the solver diagram](<../assets/images/semi_implicit.png>).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial D}{\partial\tau}
&=R_D^{\rm exp}-\nabla_h^2(\Phi_{\rm lin}+T_0\pi)-\nabla_h^2\Phi_s,\\
\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\tau}
&=-\mathcal A^{\rm exp}-\int_0^1D\,d\sigma,\\
\frac{\partial T'}{\partial\tau}
&=R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
-\dot\sigma_L\frac{\partial T_0}{\partial\sigma}
+\kappa T_0W_L,\\
\Phi_{\rm lin}&=GT'.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.1}
$$

where

$$
\dot\sigma_L(\sigma)
=\sigma\int_0^1D\,d\sigma'
-\int_0^\sigma D\,d\sigma',
\qquad
W_L(\sigma)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_0^\sigma D\,d\sigma'.
\tag{2.5.2}
$$

The explicit inputs \(\mathcal A^{\rm exp}\), \(R_D^{\rm exp}\), and \(R_{T'}^{\rm exp}\)
are evaluated at the current time level \(j\). Here
\(\mathcal A^{\rm exp}=\int_0^1(\mathbf v_h\!\cdot\!\nabla_h\pi)\,d\sigma\).
The grid-point stage forms \(K+\delta\Phi\), whose contribution to \(R_D^{\rm exp}\)
after spectral analysis is \(-\nabla_h^2(K+\delta\Phi)\).

Equation (2.5.23) applies time centering to the linear geopotential response
\(\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}=G\overline{\mathbf T'}\) and carries the fixed terrain
forcing separately.

Divergence drives two linear feedback loops.

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&\longrightarrow \int_0^1D\,d\sigma
\longrightarrow \pi
\longrightarrow T_0\pi
\longrightarrow D,\\
D&\longrightarrow(\dot\sigma_L,W_L)
\longrightarrow T'
\longrightarrow\Phi_{\rm lin}
\longrightarrow D.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.3}
$$

The vorticity equation lies in neither of these two linear gravity-wave feedback loops, so it
continues to be advanced with the explicit leapfrog scheme.

## 2. What is actually solved in one time step

Let \(j\) be the current level at which the explicit tendencies are evaluated,
\(\widetilde{Q}^{\,j-1}\) the old level that has already passed through the time filter, and
\(Q_*^{j+1}\) the candidate new level before physical processes are added. The unknown of the
semi-implicit step is not the usual divergence tendency but the time-centered divergence
between the old level and the candidate new level:

$$
\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI}^{\,j}
=\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf D}^{\,j-1}+\mathbf D_*^{j+1}}{2}.
\tag{2.5.4}
$$

At run time, the following operations are carried out in order for each spherical harmonic
coefficient \((n,m)\):

1. assemble an \(L\)-dimensional right-hand side from the filtered old-level state and the
   explicit current-level tendencies;
2. solve one \(L\times L\) vertical linear system to obtain
   \(\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI}^{\,j}\);
3. complete the linear column-mass and temperature tendencies using the same divergence
   solution;
4. recover the candidate new-level divergence from the centered divergence, and then update
   temperature and \(\pi\) with the leapfrog formulas.

Under the spherical harmonic transform, \(-\nabla_h^2\) becomes the scalar
\(\lambda_n=n(n+1)\) for total wavenumber \(n\). Different \((n,m)\) therefore decouple, and
all zonal wavenumbers \(m\) with the same \(n\) can share one vertical solve matrix. We first
construct the required vertical operators, and then derive this linear system and its
right-hand side in full.

## 3. Matrix representation of the vertical operators { #vertical-operator-matrices }

Take an arbitrary full-level variable
\(X\). Computing \(L\) output
values from the \(L\) input values of one air column can always be written as

$$
Y_k=\sum_{j=1}^{L}(\mathsf L_\sigma)_{kj}X_j,
\qquad
\mathbf Y=\mathsf L_\sigma\mathbf X.
\tag{2.5.5}
$$

Row \(k\) of the vertical operator matrix \(\mathsf L_\sigma\) answers "how is layer \(k\)
computed from the whole column", and column \(j\) answers "which target levels does the input
at layer \(j\) affect". We now explain where the three most important vertical matrices in
PlaSiC, \(G,C,\mathcal T_D\), come from.

### 3.1 Hydrostatic integration matrix { #hydrostatic-integration-matrix }

Number the vertical layers from the model top to the surface as \(1,2,\ldots,L\), and define

$$
\ell_j
=\ln\frac{\sigma_{j+1/2}}{\sigma_{j-1/2}},
\qquad j\ge 2.
\tag{2.5.6}
$$

The dimensionless hydrostatic equation integrated over the \(j\)-th full layer, gives

$$
\Phi_{j-1/2}
=\Phi_{j+1/2}+\ell_jT_{v,j}.
\tag{2.5.7}
$$

In words, moving upward across one complete layer adds that layer's \(\ell_jT_{v,j}\) to the
geopotential. However, the full-level value \(\Phi_j\) is neither the lower-interface value
\(\Phi_{j+1/2}\) nor a simple arithmetic mean of the two interface geopotentials. It is
defined as the mass-weighted average of \(\Phi\) over layer \(j\) with respect to σ. Let

$$
a=\sigma_{j-1/2},
\qquad
b=\sigma_{j+1/2},
\qquad
\Delta\sigma_j=b-a.
$$

Since the air mass per unit horizontal area in a σ layer is proportional to \(d\sigma\), the
layer average is

$$
\Phi_j
=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b\Phi(\sigma)\,d\sigma.
$$

Within layer \(j\), adopt the layer-constant approximation \(T_v(\sigma)=T_{v,j}\).
Integrating the hydrostatic equation from an arbitrary position \(\sigma\) in the layer down
to the lower interface \(b\) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(\sigma)-\Phi_{j+1/2}
&=\int_{\sigma}^{b}T_{v,j}\,d\ln\sigma'\\
&=T_{v,j}\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting this back into the definition of the layer average:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_j
&=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b
\left(\Phi_{j+1/2}
+T_{v,j}\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}\right)d\sigma\\
&=\Phi_{j+1/2}
+\underbrace{\left[
\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}\,d\sigma
\right]}_{\alpha_j}T_{v,j}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\int\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}\,d\sigma
=\sigma\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}+\sigma,
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_j
&=\frac{1}{b-a}
\left[\sigma\ln\frac{b}{\sigma}+\sigma\right]_a^b\\[2pt]
&=\frac{b-a-a\ln(b/a)}{b-a}\\[2pt]
&=1-\frac{\sigma_{j-1/2}}{\Delta\sigma_j}
\ln\frac{\sigma_{j+1/2}}{\sigma_{j-1/2}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.8}
$$

Here \(\alpha_jT_{v,j}\) is exactly **the contribution of this layer's own temperature to this
layer's mean geopotential**.

For the top layer the upper bound is \(\sigma_{1/2}=0\), where \(\ln 0\) cannot be evaluated
directly. Taking the limit \(\sigma_{1/2}\to0\) in (2.5.8) gives \(\alpha_1=1\), and the
source code sets this value directly.

Thus the geopotential of layer \(k\) relative to the surface equals "the partial contribution
of its own layer" plus "the contributions of all complete layers below it":

$$
\Phi_k-\Phi_s
=\alpha_kT_{v,k}
+\sum_{j=k+1}^{L}\ell_jT_{v,j}
=\sum_{j=1}^{L}G_{kj}T_{v,j}.
\tag{2.5.9}
$$

The corresponding matrix elements are

$$
G_{kj}=
\begin{cases}
0, & j<k,\\[2pt]
\alpha_k, & j=k,\\[2pt]
\ell_j, & j>k.
\end{cases}
\tag{2.5.10}
$$

For example, with only three layers,

$$
G=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & \ell_2 & \ell_3\\
0 & \alpha_2 & \ell_3\\
0 & 0 & \alpha_3
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.5.11}
$$

It is upper triangular, because the geopotential of layer \(k\) is determined by the
temperature thicknesses of layer \(k\) and the layers **below** it; temperatures above never
affect it. The more accurate vector form should be written as

$$
\boldsymbol\Phi-\Phi_s\mathbf 1=G\mathbf T_v,
\tag{2.5.12}
$$

rather than simply \(\boldsymbol\Phi=G\mathbf T_v\), which would drop the surface
geopotential.

### 3.2 C matrix

First clarify what \(G\) and \(C\) each have to do.

* \(G\) turns the layer virtual temperatures \(\mathbf T_v\) into the layer geopotentials
  \(\boldsymbol\Phi-\Phi_s\mathbf 1\). Computing the geopotential of layer \(k\) requires
  integrating downward from that layer to the surface, so \(G\) is upper triangular.
* The \(\omega/p\) of equation (2.1.15), on the other hand, contains an integral from the
  model top to the current layer; the key part of its continuous form is
  \(\sigma^{-1}\int_0^\sigma A\,d\sigma'\). It needs a top-down accumulation operator, that
  is, one whose direction is opposite to that of \(G\).

From this "direction of computation" point of view, it might seem sufficient to write a
separate lower-triangular summation matrix. However, the weights of \(C\) cannot be decided by
integration accuracy alone: they must also remain consistent with \(G\). To understand this
constraint, we first unfold the energy conversion in the continuous equations.

We now transfer the continuous energy ledger of to the σ-level discretization.

Let

$$
W_\sigma=\operatorname{diag}
(\Delta\sigma_1,\ldots,\Delta\sigma_L)
$$

be the **layer mass-weight matrix**. The factor \(p_s/g\) common to all layers is omitted,
because it cancels when two vertical operators are compared. For any two layer variables
\(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf y\),

$$
\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma\mathbf y
=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Delta\sigma_kx_ky_k
$$

is the discrete integral over the whole column.

We now derive the form of the fixed matrix \(C\) step by step.

Consider first the horizontal momentum equation at layer \(k\). The pressure-gradient
acceleration produced by the geopotential perturbation is \(-\nabla_h\Phi'_k\). The rate of
change of the kinetic energy per unit mass, \(K_k=\tfrac12\lvert\mathbf v_{h,k}\rvert^2\),
equals the velocity dotted with the acceleration, so the contribution of the pressure
gradient to the kinetic energy tendency is

$$
\left.\frac{\partial K_k}{\partial\tau}\right|_{\Phi'}
=-\mathbf v_{h,k}\cdot\nabla_h\Phi'_k.
$$

Using the product rule identity

$$
\nabla_h\cdot(\Phi'_k\mathbf v_{h,k})
=\mathbf v_{h,k}\cdot\nabla_h\Phi'_k
+\Phi'_k\underbrace{\nabla_h\cdot\mathbf v_{h,k}}_{D_k},
$$

we can rewrite the pressure-gradient work as

$$
-\mathbf v_{h,k}\cdot\nabla_h\Phi'_k
=-\nabla_h\cdot(\Phi'_k\mathbf v_{h,k})+\Phi'_kD_k.
$$

The first term on the right is just the flux divergence that transports kinetic energy
horizontally; the second term, \(\Phi'_kD_k\), is the conversion term between pressure work
and divergence at this layer.

$$
\int_{\mathbb S^2}
-\nabla_h\cdot(\Phi'_k\mathbf v_{h,k})\,dA=0.
$$

Therefore the globally integrated pressure work at layer \(k\) can be written as

$$
\int_{\mathbb S^2}\Phi'_kD_k\,dA.
$$

Next, sum in the vertical direction. The mass weight of the \(k\)-th σ layer is proportional
to \(\Delta\sigma_k\), so this part of the kinetic energy tendency in the whole-column,
global integral is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Delta\sigma_k
\int_{\mathbb S^2}D_k\Phi'_k\,dA
&=\int_{\mathbb S^2}
\mathbf D^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma\boldsymbol\Phi'\,dA\\
&=\int_{\mathbb S^2}
\mathbf D^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma(G\mathbf T)\,dA.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is where \(\mathbf D^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma(G\mathbf T)\) comes from.

In the linear discretization, the divergence produces the pressure-vertical-velocity variable
\(\mathbf W_{\rm lin}\equiv(\omega/p)_{\rm lin}=-C\mathbf D\) (defined in (2.5.17)). After
dropping common constants such as \(R_d\) on both sides, the compressional work in the thermal
budget is

$$
\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma\mathbf W_{\rm lin}
=-\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma C\mathbf D.
$$

Therefore the sum of the discrete pressure work on the kinetic and thermal sides is

$$
\mathbf D^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma G\mathbf T
-\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma C\mathbf D.
$$

For this to vanish, it must hold for arbitrary \(\mathbf T\) and \(\mathbf D\)
that (Simmons & Burridge, 1981)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf D^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma G\mathbf T
&=\mathbf T^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma C\mathbf D.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.13}
$$

The matrices themselves must therefore satisfy

$$
W_\sigma C=G^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma,
\qquad
C=W_\sigma^{-1}G^{\mathsf T}W_\sigma.
\tag{2.5.14}
$$

Note that \(C\) is not the inverse of \(G\): it does not recover temperature from geopotential;
it pairs the vertical action in the other equation with \(G\).

Writing out (2.5.14) in component form at row \(k\), column \(j\) gives

$$
C_{kj}
=\frac{\Delta\sigma_j}{\Delta\sigma_k}G_{jk}.
\tag{2.5.15}
$$

Substituting the three-layer matrix (2.5.11) into (2.5.15), a general non-uniform grid gives

$$
C=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0\\
\dfrac{\Delta\sigma_1}{\Delta\sigma_2}\ell_2
& \alpha_2 & 0\\[8pt]
\dfrac{\Delta\sigma_1}{\Delta\sigma_3}\ell_3
& \dfrac{\Delta\sigma_2}{\Delta\sigma_3}\ell_3
& \alpha_3
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.5.16}
$$

The matrix can now be read directly: layer 1 depends only on layer 1, near the model top;
layer 2 depends on layers 1 and 2; layer 3 depends on layers 1, 2, and 3. This is exactly
accumulation from the model top down to the current layer, so \(C\) is lower triangular. If
the three layers have equal thickness, every thickness ratio is 1, and only then is
\(C=G^{\mathsf T}\).

### 3.3 The vertical matrix from divergence to the linear temperature tendency

State the conclusion first: \(\mathcal T_D\) turns the whole-column divergence profile

$$
\mathbf D=(D_1,D_2,\ldots,D_L)^{\mathsf T}
$$

into a **linear temperature response** at each layer. Row \(k\) answers "how is the temperature
of layer \(k\) affected by the divergence of the whole column", and column \(j\) answers
"which layers' temperatures does the divergence at layer \(j\) affect". What actually enters
the right-hand side of the temperature equation is \(-\mathcal T_D\mathbf D\), not
\(\mathcal T_D\mathbf D\).

The reason why "the divergence at layer \(j\) affects the temperature at many layers" is that
the divergence acts on temperature through two paths:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Path one:}\quad
\mathbf D&\longrightarrow\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}
\longrightarrow
-\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}\frac{\partial T_0}{\partial\sigma},\\
\text{Path two:}\quad
\mathbf D&\longrightarrow \mathcal W_{\mathrm{lin}}
\longrightarrow \kappa T_0\mathcal W_{\mathrm{lin}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Path one is vertical motion carrying the reference temperature profile; path two is adiabatic
compression or expansion caused by convergence or divergence of the air column. We derive the
two paths separately below, and then combine them into a single matrix.

The semi-implicit algorithm extracts only the fast linear gravity-wave coupling near the
reference state. Take the reference state to be at rest with a horizontally uniform surface
pressure, and denote the order of magnitude of a perturbation quantity by \(\varepsilon\).
Then \(\mathbf v_h=O(\varepsilon)\) and \(\nabla_h\pi=O(\varepsilon)\), so

$$
P=\mathbf v_h\cdot\nabla_h\pi=O(\varepsilon^2).
$$

That is, \(P\) is the product of two perturbation quantities and is dropped in the linear part.
Thus \(A=D+P\) reduces at linear order to

$$
A_{\mathrm{lin}}=D.
$$

It then follows that

$$
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}(\sigma)
=\sigma\int_0^1D\,d\sigma'
-\int_0^\sigma D\,d\sigma',
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}_{\mathrm{lin}}
\equiv\left(\frac{\boldsymbol\omega}{\mathbf p}\right)_{\mathrm{lin}}
\;\widehat{=}\;-C\mathbf D.
\tag{2.5.17}
$$

Substituting \(T=T_0+T'\) into the two vertical terms of the thermodynamic equation gives

$$
-\dot\sigma\frac{\partial(T_0+T')}{\partial\sigma}
+\kappa(T_0+T')\mathcal W.
$$

Since \(\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}\), \(\mathcal W_{\mathrm{lin}}\), and \(T'\) are all
first-order perturbations, \(\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}\partial_\sigma T'\) and
\(T'\mathcal W_{\mathrm{lin}}\) are second-order small quantities. The linear part retains
only the background temperature \(T_0\):

$$
\mathbf R_T^{\mathrm{lin}}
=-\boldsymbol{\dot\sigma}_{\mathrm{lin}}\odot
\frac{\partial\mathbf T_0}{\partial\sigma}
+\kappa\mathbf T_0\odot\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}_{\mathrm{lin}}.
\tag{2.5.18}
$$

Here \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication at the same layer.

Consider an interior interface \(h=r+1/2\) between layers \(r\) and \(r+1\). 

Writing the two integrals of (2.5.17) as sums over
layer thicknesses gives

$$
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},h}
=\sigma_h\sum_{j=1}^{L}\Delta\sigma_jD_j
-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\Delta\sigma_jD_j.
$$

Collecting the coefficients of each \(D_j\) gives

$$
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},h}
=\sum_{j=1}^{L}S_{hj}D_j,
\qquad
S_{hj}=
\begin{cases}
\Delta\sigma_j(\sigma_h-1), & j\le r,\\[2pt]
\Delta\sigma_j\sigma_h, & j>r,
\end{cases}
\qquad h=r+\tfrac12.
\tag{2.5.19}
$$

The matrix \(S\) is only a bookkeeping device for the derivation; the source code does not
need to store it separately. Each of its rows says how to obtain the \(\dot\sigma\) at one
half-level from the whole-column \(\mathbf D\).

For the \(k\)-th full layer,
[grid-point dynamics equation (2.4.15)](<2.4-sigma-vertical-discretization.md#vertical-advection-layer-difference>)


$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}
\frac{\partial T_0}{\partial\sigma}
\right)_k
&=
\frac{
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},k-1/2}\delta T_{0,k-1/2}
+\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},k+1/2}\delta T_{0,k+1/2}}
{2\Delta\sigma_k}.
\end{aligned}
$$


Next substitute

$$
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},k-1/2}
=\sum_{j=1}^{L}S_{k-1/2,j}D_j,
\qquad
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},k+1/2}
=\sum_{j=1}^{L}S_{k+1/2,j}D_j.
$$

Substituting term by term and collecting the coefficients of the same \(D_j\):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(
\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}
\frac{\partial T_0}{\partial\sigma}
\right)_k
&=
\frac{1}{2\Delta\sigma_k}
\left[
\delta T_{0,k-1/2}
\sum_{j=1}^{L}S_{k-1/2,j}D_j
+
\delta T_{0,k+1/2}
\sum_{j=1}^{L}S_{k+1/2,j}D_j
\right]\\[3pt]
&=\sum_{j=1}^{L}
\underbrace{
\frac{
\delta T_{0,k-1/2}S_{k-1/2,j}
+\delta T_{0,k+1/2}S_{k+1/2,j}}
{2\Delta\sigma_k}
}_{\Gamma_{kj}}
D_j\\[3pt]
&=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\Gamma_{kj}D_j
=(\Gamma\mathbf D)_k,\\[3pt]
\Gamma_{kj}
&=\frac{
\delta T_{0,k-1/2}S_{k-1/2,j}
+\delta T_{0,k+1/2}S_{k+1/2,j}}
{2\Delta\sigma_k}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.20}
$$

At the top layer the temperature jump at the missing upper boundary is set to zero; at the
bottom layer the temperature jump at the missing lower boundary is also set to zero. Therefore:

* the top layer uses only \(\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},3/2}\) at its lower interface;
* interior layers use both the upper and lower interfaces;
* the bottom layer uses only \(\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{lin},L-1/2}\) at its upper interface.

Equation (2.5.20) is the complete definition of \(\Gamma\). Here \(\Gamma_{kj}\) measures how
much the divergence at layer \(j\) affects layer \(k\) through transport of the reference
temperature profile.


Path two is more direct. Equation (2.5.17) already gave
\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}_{\mathrm{lin}}=-C\mathbf D\), so

$$
\kappa\mathbf T_0\odot\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}_{\mathrm{lin}}
=-\operatorname{diag}(\kappa\mathbf T_0)C\mathbf D.
$$

Substituting both paths back into (2.5.18) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf R_T^{\mathrm{lin}}
&=-\Gamma\mathbf D
-\operatorname{diag}(\kappa\mathbf T_0)C\mathbf D\\
&=-\underbrace{\left[
\Gamma+\operatorname{diag}(\kappa\mathbf T_0)C
\right]}_{\mathcal T_D}\mathbf D
=-\mathcal T_D\mathbf D,\\[3pt]
(\mathcal T_D)_{kj}
&=\Gamma_{kj}+\kappa(T_0)_kC_{kj}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.21}
$$

The matrix elements can now be interpreted precisely: \((\mathcal T_D)_{kj}\) is the coupling
coefficient from the divergence at layer \(j\) to the **negative temperature tendency
operator** at layer \(k\).

Pay special attention to the minus sign outside. The program array `vertical_tau` stores
\(\mathcal T_D\); when the temperature tendency is actually updated, the code applies
\(-\mathcal T_D\mathbf D\).

### 3.4 What the matrix formulation actually buys

These three matrices act only on the vertical levels of a single air column; they never mix
different latitude–longitude grid points. For every horizontal grid point, or every spherical
harmonic coefficient \((n,m)\), the program reuses the same set of vertical matrices:

| Mathematical object | Source array | Input \(\rightarrow\) output |
|---|---|---|
| \(G\) | `vertical_g` | \(\mathbf T_v\rightarrow\boldsymbol\Phi-\Phi_s\mathbf1\) | 
| \(C\) | `vertical_c` | $\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}_{\mathrm{lin}} \equiv\left(\frac{\boldsymbol\omega}{\mathbf p}\right)_{\mathrm{lin}} \;\widehat{=}\;-C\mathbf D.$ | 
| \(\mathcal T_D\) | `vertical_tau` | \(\mathbf D\rightarrow-\mathbf R_T^{\mathrm{lin}}\) | 

## 4. Solving for centered divergence and updating the state

### 4.1 Centered values over the leapfrog interval

For any variable \(Q\), define

$$
\delta_\tau Q^j
=\frac{Q_*^{j+1}-\widetilde Q^{\,j-1}}{2\Delta\tau},
\qquad
\overline Q^{\,j}
=\frac{Q_*^{j+1}+\widetilde Q^{\,j-1}}{2}
=\widetilde Q^{\,j-1}+\Delta\tau\,\delta_\tau Q^j.
\tag{2.5.22}
$$

Thus \(2\Delta\tau\) is the full leapfrog interval from the old level to the candidate new
level, while \(\Delta\tau\) is the distance from the old level to the time center. To shorten
the formulas below, define \(Q^-\equiv\widetilde Q^{\,j-1}\) and drop the current-level
superscript \(j\) on the explicit tendencies.

Define the column-integration row vector that acts in the surface pressure equation:

$$
L_p\equiv\Delta\boldsymbol\sigma^{\mathsf T}
=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\sigma_1&\cdots&\Delta\sigma_L\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here

$$
\Delta\sigma_k
=\sigma_{k+1/2}-\sigma_{k-1/2},
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Delta\sigma_k=1.
$$

Thus \(L_p\) takes a whole column of divergence as input and returns a scalar:

$$
\boxed{
L_p\overline{\mathbf D}
=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Delta\sigma_k\overline D_k
\;\approx\;
\int_0^1\overline D(\sigma)\,d\sigma
}
$$

That is, \(L_p:\mathbb R^L\rightarrow\mathbb R\) only compresses the layered divergences into
the σ-weighted divergence of the whole column. 

This notation comes directly from the linear term in the continuous column mass equation

$$
\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\tau}
\supset
-\int_0^1D\,d\sigma,
$$

whose discrete form is \(-L_p\mathbf D\).

Applying (2.5.22) to the divergence, continuity, and thermodynamic equations in (2.5.1),
and using the vertical operators from Section 3, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\mathbf D}
&=\mathbf D^-
+\Delta\tau
\left[
\mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}
-\nabla_h^2
\left(\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}+\mathbf T_0\overline\pi\right)
-(\nabla_h^2\Phi_s)\mathbf 1
\right],\\
\overline\pi
&=\pi^-
-\Delta\tau
\left(\mathcal A^{\rm exp}+L_p\overline{\mathbf D}\right),\\
\overline{\mathbf T'}
&=\mathbf T'^-
+\Delta\tau
\left(\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
-\mathcal T_D\overline{\mathbf D}\right),\\
\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}
&=G\overline{\mathbf T'}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.23}
$$

Here \(\mathbf 1\) is the \(L\)-component vector of ones, so the prescribed terrain forcing
\(-\nabla_h^2\Phi_s\) acts equally at every level. Substituting the third equation of (2.5.23)
into the fourth gives the linear geopotential response to the centered temperature perturbation.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}
&=G\overline{\mathbf T'}\\
&=G\left[
      \mathbf T'^-
      +\Delta\tau
       \left(\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
       -\mathcal T_D\overline{\mathbf D}\right)
    \right]\\
&=\underbrace{
   G\mathbf T'^-
   +\Delta\tau G\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
  }_{\text{known part of the linear geopotential}}
  -\Delta\tau G\mathcal T_D\overline{\mathbf D}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term is the thermal feedback: the centered divergence first produces a temperature
change through \(\mathcal T_D\), and then a geopotential change through the hydrostatic
integration matrix \(G\).

For the surface pressure term, substituting the second equation of (2.5.23) into
\(\mathbf T_0\overline\pi\) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf T_0\overline\pi
&=\mathbf T_0
  \left[
    \pi^--\Delta\tau
    \left(\mathcal A^{\rm exp}
    +L_p\overline{\mathbf D}\right)
  \right]\\
&=\mathbf T_0\pi^-
  -\Delta\tau\mathbf T_0\mathcal A^{\rm exp}
  -\Delta\tau\mathbf T_0
   \left(L_p\overline{\mathbf D}\right)\\
&=\underbrace{
   \mathbf T_0\pi^-
   -\Delta\tau\mathbf T_0\mathcal A^{\rm exp}
  }_{\text{known surface pressure contribution}}
  -\Delta\tau(\mathbf T_0L_p)\overline{\mathbf D}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step uses only associativity of matrix multiplication,
\(\mathbf T_0(L_p\overline{\mathbf D})
=(\mathbf T_0L_p)\overline{\mathbf D}\). Here

$$
\mathbf T_0\in\mathbb R^{L\times1},
\qquad
L_p\in\mathbb R^{1\times L},
\qquad
\mathbf T_0L_p\in\mathbb R^{L\times L}.
$$

Adding the two expressions above, all terms containing the unknown centered divergence
combine exactly into

$$
-\Delta\tau
\left(G\mathcal T_D+\mathbf T_0L_p\right)
\overline{\mathbf D}.
$$

We therefore define the vertical feedback operator

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal K
\equiv
G\mathcal T_D+\mathbf T_0L_p
}
\tag{2.5.24}
$$

Collect the known parts of \(\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}\) and
\(\mathbf T_0\overline\pi\) in

$$
\mathbf H
\equiv
G\mathbf T'^-
+\mathbf T_0\pi^-
+\Delta\tau
\left(
G\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
-\mathbf T_0\mathcal A^{\rm exp}
\right).
$$

The sum of the centered linear geopotential and pressure terms is then

$$
\overline{\boldsymbol\Phi}_{\rm lin}+\mathbf T_0\overline\pi
=\mathbf H-\Delta\tau\mathcal K\overline{\mathbf D}.
$$

Substituting this sum into the first equation of (2.5.23) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\mathbf D}
&=\mathbf D^-
 +\Delta\tau
  \left[
    \mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}
    -\nabla_h^2
     \left(
       \mathbf H-\Delta\tau
       \mathcal K\overline{\mathbf D}
     \right)
    -(\nabla_h^2\Phi_s)\mathbf 1
  \right]\\
&=\mathbf D^-
 +\Delta\tau
  \left(
    \mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}-\nabla_h^2\mathbf H
    -(\nabla_h^2\Phi_s)\mathbf 1
  \right)
 +\Delta\tau^2
  \nabla_h^2
  \left(\mathcal K\overline{\mathbf D}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Moving the last term, which still contains \(\overline{\mathbf D}\), to the left-hand side
gives the closed linear equation

$$
\overline{\mathbf D}
-\Delta\tau^2\nabla_h^2
 \left(\mathcal K\overline{\mathbf D}\right)
=\mathbf D^-
+\Delta\tau
  \left(
    \mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}-\nabla_h^2\mathbf H
    -(\nabla_h^2\Phi_s)\mathbf 1
  \right).
$$

### 4.2 The vertical linear system in spherical harmonic space

For a spherical harmonic total wavenumber \(n>0\),

$$
-\nabla_h^2\longleftrightarrow\lambda_n,
\qquad
\lambda_n=n(n+1).
$$

Therefore, for a single \((n,m)\) mode, the closed equation above first becomes

$$
\left(I+\lambda_n\Delta\tau^2\mathcal K\right)
\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI,n}^m
=\left(\mathbf D_n^m\right)^-
+\Delta\tau
 \left[
   \left(\mathbf R_D^{\rm exp}\right)_n^m
   +\lambda_n\left(\mathbf H_n^m+\Phi_{s,n}^m\mathbf 1\right)
 \right].
$$

Finally, dividing the whole equation by \(\lambda_n\) gives

$$
M_n\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI,n}^m=\mathbf b_n^m,
\tag{2.5.25}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
M_n
=\frac{1}{\lambda_n}I
+\Delta\tau^2
\left(
G\mathcal T_D+\mathbf T_0L_p
\right)
}
\tag{2.5.26}
$$

and the complete right-hand side is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf b_n^m
={}&\frac{1}{\lambda_n}(\mathbf D_n^m)^-\\
&+\Delta\tau
\Bigg[
\Phi_{s,n}^m\mathbf1
+G(\mathbf T'_n{}^m)^-
+\mathbf T_0(\pi_n^m)^-
+\frac{1}{\lambda_n}(\mathbf R_D^{\rm exp})_n^m\\
&\hspace{3.8em}
+\Delta\tau
\left(
G(\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp})_n^m
-\mathbf T_0(\mathcal A^{\rm exp})_n^m
\right)
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{2.5.27}
$$

The prescribed terrain enters (2.5.27) through \(\Phi_{s,n}^m\mathbf 1\). All terms in
\(\mathbf b_n^m\) are known before the linear solve.

At initialization, `makebm` constructs (2.5.26) for every \(n=1,\ldots,N\), inverts it, and
caches

$$
B_n=M_n^{-1}.
\tag{2.5.28}
$$

The inversion is performed numerically by LU decomposition (Golub & Van Loan, 2013;
Anderson et al., 1999); see the appendix
[Matrix inversion](../9-appendix/9.1-matrix-inversion.md).

At run time, all \(m\) with the same total wavenumber \(n\) share \(B_n\), so only the
matrix–vector product

$$
\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI,n}^m=B_n\mathbf b_n^m.
\tag{2.5.29}
$$

is needed. Here \(B_n\) is the vertical solve matrix, not the Bernoulli scalar of
the dynamical equations. \(M_n\), \(B_n\), \(G\), \(C\), and \(\mathcal T_D\) are all of
dimension \(L\times L\).

!!! note "Why \(n=0\) does not enter the same solver"

    \(n=0\) is the globally constant mode, \(\lambda_0=0\), so there is no invertible
    horizontal Laplacian. It does not represent a horizontally propagating gravity wave
    either. The dynamical core holds the global mean \(\pi\) fixed as the mass reference and
    sets the corresponding centered divergence to zero, which prevents numerical drift of the
    total mass.

### 4.3 How the solution restores the full tendencies and the candidate new level { #semi-implicit-correction }

Once the centered divergence is known, the full adiabatic tendencies of the continuity and
thermodynamic equations are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A
&=\mathcal A^{\rm exp}
+L_p\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI},\\
\mathbf R_{T'}
&=\mathbf R_{T'}^{\rm exp}
-\mathcal T_D\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI}
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.30}
$$

Note that \(\mathcal A=-\partial\pi/\partial\tau\); it is not the positive tendency of
\(\pi\). The candidate new level is then recovered as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf D_*^{j+1}
&=2\overline{\mathbf D}_{\rm SI}^{\,j}
-\widetilde{\mathbf D}^{\,j-1},\\
\mathbf T'_*{}^{j+1}
&=\widetilde{\mathbf T'}{}^{\,j-1}
+2\Delta\tau\,\mathbf R_{T'}^j,\\
\pi_*^{j+1}
&=\widetilde\pi^{\,j-1}-2\Delta\tau\,\mathcal A^j
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.5.31}
$$
