# 2.1 Governing Equations { #plasic }

This note is the entry point of the "Dynamical Core" chapter: Section 1 lists the governing equations that the core actually solves, and [2.2 Ten-Step Algorithm](<2.2-ten-step-algorithm.md>) then defines its responsibilities and surveys the ten steps of one time step in terms of representation space and time level. The spectral–grid transform, vertical discretization, semi-implicit solve, coupling to physical processes, and numerical dissipation are derived further in the companion notes. Regarding the derivation of these equations, we provide them directly and omit the full derivation, as this falls outside the scope of a course on numerical modeling. Interested readers may refer to An Introduction to Dynamic Meteorology by Holton.

!!! tip "The main thread to remember first"

    PlaSiC splits the same set of moist primitive equations across the representation spaces that suit each term best: grid space computes nonlinear products and column-wise vertical transport, spectral space computes horizontal derivatives, the fast gravity-wave coupling, and scale-selective dissipation, and the physical parameterizations use a dimensional grid state recovered from the spectral state. The parts are closed by a leapfrog time step with Robert–Asselin filtering.

## 1. The governing equations

### 1.1 Basic setup { #11 }

Let

$$
\lambda=\text{longitude},\qquad
\varphi=\text{latitude},\qquad
\mu=\sin\varphi .
$$

The spherical area element in \((\lambda,\mu)\) coordinates is

$$
dA=d\lambda\,d\mu
$$.

The model integrates dimensionless equations. Time is measured in units of \(\Omega^{-1}\), where \(\Omega\) is the planetary rotation rate, and horizontal length in units of the planetary radius \(a\). The basic scales are

$$
t_*=\Omega^{-1},\qquad
u_*=a\Omega,\qquad
T_*=\frac{a^2\Omega^2}{R_d},
$$

where \(R_d\) is the gas constant of dry air, and the dimensionless time is \(\tau=\Omega t\). Winds and temperatures below are expressed in units of \(u_*\) and \(T_*\).

Let \(\mathbf v_h=(u,v)\) be the horizontal physical wind. The model does not use \((u,v)\) directly in every formula; instead it introduces the Robert wind (Robert, 1966):

$$
U=u\cos\varphi,\qquad V=v\cos\varphi .
\tag{2.1.1}
$$

The terrain-following \(\sigma\) coordinate (Phillips, 1957) is defined as

$$
\sigma=\frac{p}{p_s(\lambda,\varphi,t)},
\tag{2.1.2}
$$

where \(p\) is pressure and \(p_s\) is the surface pressure, which varies with position and time. Thus \(\sigma=0\) corresponds to the model top and \(\sigma=1\) to the surface. The actual pressure on a given \(\sigma\) surface is \(p=\sigma p_s\), so the lower boundary always lies at the fixed value \(\sigma=1\).

### 1.2 Full prognostic equations and diagnostic relations

The core prognostic variables on each full \(\sigma\) level are:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(\zeta\) | relative vorticity |
| \(D\) | horizontal divergence |
| \(T'=T-T_0\) | temperature perturbation relative to the reference profile \(T_0(\sigma)\) |
| \(\pi=\ln(p_s/p_0)\) | dimensionless log surface pressure; \(p_0\) is a fixed reference pressure |
| \(\chi=(p_s/p_0)q\) | mass-weighted humidity variable |

In this section, symbols without hats denote the continuous fields themselves; no grid or spectral representation has been chosen yet. From [2.2 Ten-Step Algorithm](<2.2-ten-step-algorithm.md>) onward, where the discrete algorithm begins, a hat \(\widehat{\phantom X}\) denotes a spherical harmonic coefficient, and the subscript \({\rm gp}\) denotes a value on the Gaussian grid.

All equations below use the model's internal dimensionless variables and the dimensionless time \(\tau\), and horizontal derivatives are taken on constant-\(\sigma\) surfaces. The five prognostic equations are the vorticity equation, the divergence equation, the column mass equation, the temperature perturbation equation, and the mass-weighted humidity equation (Haltiner & Williams, 1982; Hoskins & Simmons, 1975; White et al., 2005):

$$
\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial\tau}
=\frac{1}{1-\mu^2}\frac{\partial F_V}{\partial\lambda}
-\frac{\partial F_U}{\partial\mu}+P_\zeta,
\tag{2.1.3}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial D}{\partial\tau}
=\frac{1}{1-\mu^2}\frac{\partial F_U}{\partial\lambda}
+\frac{\partial F_V}{\partial\mu}
-\nabla_h^2 B+P_D,
\tag{2.1.4}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\tau}
=-\int_0^1 A\,d\sigma,
\tag{2.1.5}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial T'}{\partial\tau}
=F_T-\dot\sigma\frac{\partial T}{\partial\sigma}
+\kappa T\frac{\omega}{p}+P_T,
\tag{2.1.6}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial\tau}
+\nabla_h\cdot(\chi\mathbf v_h)
+\frac{\partial(\chi\dot\sigma)}{\partial\sigma}
=\frac{p_s}{p_0}S_q.
\tag{2.1.7}
$$

Equations (2.1.3)–(2.1.7) are prognostic. The remaining eight equations of the dynamical core are diagnostic: they do not advance any variable in time, but determine, from the current state, the closure quantities that appear on the right-hand sides of the prognostic equations.

$$
T=T_0+T',
\qquad
T_v=T\left[1+\left(\frac{R_v}{R_d}-1\right)q\right],
\qquad
q=\chi e^{-\pi}.
$$

The eight diagnostic equations are listed one by one below.

**Geopotential (hydrostatic balance).** 

$$
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\ln\sigma}=-T_v.
\tag{2.1.8}
$$

**Zonal momentum combination.**

$$
F_U
=(\zeta+f)V
-\dot\sigma\frac{\partial U}{\partial\sigma}
-T'\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\lambda}.
\tag{2.1.9}
$$

**Meridional momentum combination.**

$$
F_V
=-(\zeta+f)U
-\dot\sigma\frac{\partial V}{\partial\sigma}
-T'(1-\mu^2)\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial\mu}.
\tag{2.1.10}
$$

**Bernoulli potential.**

$$
B=K+\Phi+T_0\pi,
\qquad
K=\frac{U^2+V^2}{2(1-\mu^2)}.
\tag{2.1.11}
$$

**Horizontal temperature transport.**

$$
F_T=-\nabla_h\cdot(\mathbf v_hT')+DT'.
\tag{2.1.12}
$$

**Mass flux divergence.**

$$
A=D+\mathbf v_h\cdot\nabla_h\pi
=\frac{1}{p_s}\nabla_h\cdot(p_s\mathbf v_h).
\tag{2.1.13}
$$

**σ vertical velocity.**

$$
\dot\sigma(\sigma)
=\sigma\int_0^1A(\sigma')\,d\sigma'
-\int_0^\sigma A(\sigma')\,d\sigma'.
\tag{2.1.14}
$$

**Pressure vertical velocity.**

$$
\frac{\omega}{p}
=\mathbf v_h\cdot\nabla_h\pi
-\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_0^\sigma A(\sigma')\,d\sigma'.
\tag{2.1.15}
$$

Physical processes and numerical dissipation enter through \(P_\zeta,P_D,P_T,S_q\) in (2.1.3)–(2.1.7) and through the damping operators discussed later.

Together, (2.1.3)–(2.1.15) form a closed system of 13 equations for 13 unknowns, which [2.2 Ten-Step Algorithm](<2.2-ten-step-algorithm.md>) discretizes and implements term by term.

The five **prognostic unknowns** are advanced in time by (2.1.3)–(2.1.7):

- \(\zeta\), the relative vorticity;
- \(D\), the horizontal divergence;
- \(T'\), the temperature perturbation;
- \(\pi\), the log surface pressure;
- \(\chi\), the mass-weighted humidity.

The eight **diagnostic unknowns** are evaluated from the current state by (2.1.8)–(2.1.15), one unknown per equation:

- \(\Phi\), the geopotential;
- \(F_U\), the zonal momentum combination used to form the vorticity and divergence tendencies;
- \(F_V\), the meridional momentum combination used to form the vorticity and divergence tendencies;
- \(B\), the Bernoulli potential;
- \(F_T\), the horizontal temperature transport;
- \(A\), the mass flux divergence;
- \(\dot\sigma\), the σ vertical velocity;
- \(\omega/p\), the pressure vertical velocity.

The remaining symbols are auxiliary definitions rather than independent unknowns: \(U\) and \(V\) are the Robert winds (2.1.1); \(T\), \(T_v\), and \(q\) follow from the definitions above; and \(K\) is the kinetic energy defined together with \(B\) in (2.1.11). In the discrete algorithm, \(U\), \(V\), and \(\nabla_h\pi\) are recovered from \(\zeta\), \(D\), and \(\pi\) by the spherical harmonic transforms of [2.3](<2.3-spectral-transform-and-fft.md>), which completes the closure at every time step.
