## 1. Horizontal Diffusion: A Small-Scale Selector that Damps Only the Spectral Tail

### 1.1 Why a spectral model still needs dissipation

The spectral method represents smooth large-scale fields with high accuracy, but it cannot
represent motions smaller than the truncation wavelength. Nonlinear interactions keep transferring
kinetic energy and scalar variance toward high wavenumbers; if it piles up near the largest
representable wavenumber, the model develops unrealistic small-scale noise.

The task of horizontal diffusion is not to flatten every structure. PlaSiC uses a total-wavenumber
selector (Laursen & Eliasen, 1989; Roeckner et al., 1992):

$$
L_n=
\begin{cases}
0, & n\le n_*,\\[4pt]
\left(\dfrac{n-n_*}{N-n_*}\right)^\alpha,
& n>n_*,
\end{cases}
\tag{3.3.1}
$$

where $N$ is the spectral truncation, $n_*$ is the wavenumber at which damping starts, and
$\alpha$ controls how strongly the damping is concentrated in the spectral tail. The
normalization makes $L_N=1$, so the configured time scale can be read directly as the damping
time of the smallest resolvable scale.

### 1.2 Each spherical harmonic decays independently

For a spectral variable $\widehat X_{n,m}$, horizontal diffusion is written as

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\widehat X_{n,m}}{\partial t}\right|_H
=-r_{X,n}\widehat X_{n,m},
\qquad
r_{X,n}=\frac{L_n}{\tau_X}.
\tag{3.3.2}
$$

The zonal wavenumber $m$ determines the longitudinal structure at a given total wavenumber, but
the damping rate depends only on $n$, so structures of the same total spherical scale are treated
identically regardless of orientation. Let $\widehat X_{n,m}^{-}$ and $\widehat X_{n,m}^{+}$ be
the spectral coefficients at the beginning and the end of the update, and let $\Delta t_*$ be the
time span it covers. Backward Euler (Durran, 2010) evaluates the damping at the end-of-step state
$\widehat X_{n,m}^{+}$, hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\widehat X_{n,m}^{+}-\widehat X_{n,m}^{-}}{\Delta t_*}
&=-\frac{L_n}{\tau_X}\widehat X_{n,m}^{+},\\[4pt]
\left(1+\frac{\Delta t_*L_n}{\tau_X}\right)\widehat X_{n,m}^{+}
&=\widehat X_{n,m}^{-},\\[4pt]
\widehat X_{n,m}^{+}
&=\frac{\widehat X_{n,m}^{-}}{1+\Delta t_*L_n/\tau_X}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.3.3}
$$

Section 3 of [Spectral Dissipation](<../2-dynamical-core/2.8-spectral-dissipation.md>)
writes out the full spectral update.

### 1.3 Different variables use different time scales

Divergence, vorticity, temperature, and moisture have different small-scale cascades and
different sensitivity to numerical noise, so they use different $\tau_X$. The current C defaults
are

| Spectral variable | Damping time at the shortest wave |
|---|---:|
| Divergence $D$ | $0.20\ \mathrm d$ |
| Relative vorticity $\zeta$ | $1.10\ \mathrm d$ |
| Temperature perturbation $T'$ | $5.60\ \mathrm d$ |
| Mass-weighted moisture $\chi=(p_s/p_0)q$ | $0.10\ \mathrm d$ |

The standard T21 setup also takes $n_*=15$ and $\alpha=2$. For $N=21$, $n=18$ gives
$L_{18}=(3/6)^2=0.25$, a damping time four times longer than at the shortest wave, while
$n\le15$ is untouched by this process.

## 2. Vertical Diffusion

### 2.1 The four mixed quantities

With $K_m$ for momentum and $K_h$ for heat and moisture,

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(\rho K_m\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right),
\qquad
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}
=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(\rho K_m\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right),
\tag{3.3.4}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}
=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(\rho K_h\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}\right),
\tag{3.3.5}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left[
\rho K_h\sigma^\kappa
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z}
\right],
\qquad
\theta=T\sigma^{-\kappa}.
\tag{3.3.6}
$$

$K_m$ applies to momentum and $K_h$ to heat and moisture; both carry units of
$\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. This is the standard first-order K-closure introduced by
Louis (1979) and adopted by ECHAM (Roeckner et al., 1992).

### 2.2 Why the surface fluxes are not the lower boundary condition here

Vertical diffusion treats only the exchange between **adjacent model layers inside the
atmosphere**, so the turbulent flux $\mathcal F_{k+1/2}$ across the interface between layers $k$
and $k+1$ vanishes at both the top and the bottom:

$$
\mathcal F_{-1/2}=\mathcal F_{L+1/2}=0.
\tag{3.3.7}
$$

- The stress, sensible heat, and evaporation between the surface and the lowest layer are computed
separately by the [surface-flux parameterization](3.2-surface-fluxes.md) and are already written into
the working state as tendencies before this kernel runs.

- Zero boundary fluxes also mean that vertical
diffusion by itself only redistributes momentum and scalars within the column.

## 3. Gradient Richardson Number

### 3.1 Continuous definition

Vertical diffusion uses the gradient Richardson number (Stull, 1988; Garratt, 1992)

$$
Ri_g=
\frac{g}{\theta}
\frac{\partial\theta_v/\partial z}
{\left|\partial\boldsymbol V/\partial z\right|^2},
\qquad
\theta_v=T\left[1+\left(\frac{R_v}{R_d}-1\right)q\right]\sigma^{-\kappa}.
\tag{3.3.8}
$$

It compares two squared frequencies: the numerator approximates the buoyancy frequency $N^2$, and
the denominator is the squared vertical wind shear.

- $Ri_g<0$: virtual potential temperature decreases with height, heavy air sits above light air,
  and buoyancy directly generates turbulence;
- $Ri_g\approx0$: nearly neutral, shear dominates;
- $Ri_g>0$: buoyancy is stabilizing, but sufficiently strong shear can still maintain turbulence;
- very large $Ri_g$: the buoyancy restoring force overwhelms the shear, and gives
  very weak mixing.

Note that the bulk Richardson number compares "surface to lowest layer"; the gradient Richardson number compares "adjacent model layers".


### 3.2 From height coordinates to σ coordinates

We konw that

$$
\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial z}
=-\frac{g\sigma}{R_dT}.
\tag{3.3.9}
$$

Convert all height derivatives to σ derivatives first. For any layer variable $A$, the chain rule
gives

$$
\frac{\partial A}{\partial z}
=\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial z}\frac{\partial A}{\partial\sigma}
=-C_\sigma\frac{\partial A}{\partial\sigma},
\qquad
C_\sigma\equiv\frac{g\sigma}{R_dT}>0.
$$

Hence the virtual-potential-temperature gradient and the wind shear become

$$
\frac{\partial\theta_v}{\partial z}
=-C_\sigma\frac{\partial\theta_v}{\partial\sigma},
\qquad
\left|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol V}{\partial z}\right|^2
=C_\sigma^2
\left|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol V}{\partial\sigma}\right|^2.
$$

Substituting into (3.3.8) and canceling the common metric factor $C_\sigma$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ri_g
&=\frac{g}{\theta}
\frac{-C_\sigma\,\partial_\sigma\theta_v}
{C_\sigma^2|\partial_\sigma\boldsymbol V|^2}\\[3pt]
&=-\frac{R_dT}{\theta\sigma}
\frac{\partial_\sigma\theta_v}
{|\partial_\sigma\boldsymbol V|^2}\\[3pt]
&=-\frac{R_d\sigma^\kappa}{\sigma}
\frac{\partial_\sigma\theta_v}
{|\partial_\sigma\boldsymbol V|^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step uses $\theta=T\sigma^{-\kappa}$, i.e., $T/\theta=\sigma^\kappa$. 

Now discretize the continuous σ derivatives onto the interface between adjacent full levels $k$
and $k+1$. Define the distance between the two level centers,

$$
\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}\equiv\sigma_{k+1}-\sigma_k>0,
$$

so that

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\theta_v}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{k+1/2}
\approx
\frac{\theta_{v,k+1}-\theta_{v,k}}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}.
$$

The complete vector difference for the shear would be

$$
\left|\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol V}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{k+1/2}\right|^2
\approx
\left(\frac{u_{k+1}-u_k}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}\right)^2
+
\left(\frac{v_{k+1}-v_k}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}\right)^2.
$$

The implementation instead defines $U_k=|\boldsymbol V_k|=\sqrt{u_k^2+v_k^2}$ and approximates
the shear magnitude by the difference of the wind-speed magnitudes,
$|U_{k+1}-U_k|/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}$. The interface Richardson number is then

$$
Ri_{k+1/2}
\approx
\frac{R_d\sigma_{k+1/2}^{\kappa}}
{\sigma_{k+1/2}}
\frac{
(\theta_{v,k}-\theta_{v,k+1})/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}
}{
\left[(U_{k+1}-U_k)/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}\right]^2
}.
\tag{3.3.10}
$$

The actual computation replaces the wind difference in the denominator by
$\max(U_{\min},|U_{k+1}-U_k|)/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}$, so the nonzero floor $U_{\min}$ prevents a
division by zero when the shear vanishes. (This is the same configured floor
`minimum_wind = 1 m s⁻¹` used by the surface-flux scheme, here applied to the wind difference.)
The approximation distinguishes strong and weak wind shear robustly, but it cannot represent
vector shear with equal wind speeds and a rotating wind direction — a clear limitation of this
simplified closure.

## 4. Blackadar Mixing Length: How Large Can an Eddy Grow?

Vertical diffusion also needs a length scale for the eddies. The Blackadar (1962) form is

$$
\frac1{l_m}=\frac1{kz}+\frac1{\lambda_m},
\qquad
\frac1{l_h}=\frac1{kz}+\frac1{\lambda_h},
\tag{3.3.11}
$$

that is,

$$
l_x=\frac{\lambda_xkz}{\lambda_x+kz},
\qquad x\in\{m,h\}.
\tag{3.3.12}
$$

- For $z\ll\lambda_x/k$, $l_x\approx kz$: the surface limits the eddy size;
- for $z\gg\lambda_x/k$, $l_x\to\lambda_x$: the eddy cannot grow without bound with height.

The current parameters are

$$
\lambda_m=160\ \mathrm m,
\qquad
\lambda_h=\lambda_m\sqrt{\frac{3d}{2}}.
\tag{3.3.13}
$$

For $d=5$, $\lambda_h\approx438\ \mathrm m$. This does not mean that heat is necessarily mixed
more strongly than momentum; the final magnitude is set by multiplying with different stability
functions.


## 5. Exchange Coefficients: Mixing Length × Shear × Stability

A first-order closure writes the physical diffusivities as

$$
K_m=l_m^2S f_m(Ri_g),
\qquad
K_h=l_h^2S f_h(Ri_g),
\qquad
S=\left|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol V}{\partial z}\right|.
\tag{3.3.14}
$$

The dimensional check is direct: $l^2$ is in $\mathrm{m^2}$ and $S$ in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $K$
is in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Strong shear generates turbulence, larger eddies increase the
transport distance, and the stability functions determine how much exchange buoyancy actually
allows.

### 5.1 Unstable stratification

For $Ri_g\le0$, define the geometric factor

$$
G(l,z,\Delta z)=
\frac{l^2}{(\Delta z)^{3/2}z^{1/2}}
\left[
\left(\frac{z+\Delta z}{z}\right)^{1/3}-1
\right]^{3/2},
\tag{3.3.15}
$$

along with

$$
D_h=1+3bc\sqrt{-Ri_g}\,G(l_h,z,\Delta z),
\qquad
D_m=1+3bc\sqrt{-Ri_g}\,G(l_m,z,\Delta z).
\tag{3.3.16}
$$

The stability functions are

$$
f_h=1-\frac{3bRi_g}{D_h},
\qquad
f_m=1-\frac{2bRi_g}{D_m}.
\tag{3.3.17}
$$

Since $Ri_g<0$, both exceed one.

### 5.2 Stable stratification

For $Ri_g>0$, let $s=\sqrt{1+dRi_g}$; the current scheme uses

$$
f_h=\frac1{1+3bRi_gs},
\qquad
f_m=\frac1{1+2bRi_g/s}.
\tag{3.3.18}
$$

The default is $b=c=d=5$ (Louis, 1979; see also Roeckner et al., 1992). As $Ri_g$ grows, both functions decrease and scalar mixing is
suppressed more strongly.

## 6. Why the Finite-Layer Discretization Produces a Tridiagonal Matrix

### 6.1 The budget of one layer

When reading the interface coefficients below, keep three quantities apart:

| Quantity | Meaning | Units |
|---|---|---|
| $K_X$ | physical turbulent diffusivity in height coordinates | $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ |
| $B_X$ | coefficient obtained after rewriting the diffusion operator in $\sigma$ coordinates | $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ |
| $A^{(X)}$ | interface coupling coefficient entering the tridiagonal matrix after time and space discretization | $1$ |

$A^{(X)}$ is therefore not a new physical diffusivity: it is a **dimensionless numerical
coefficient** that combines $K_X$, the coordinate transformation, the time step, and the spacing
between adjacent levels. The next three steps show where it comes from.

#### Step 1: rewrite the height-coordinate diffusion equation in σ coordinates

For a wind component or an ordinary scalar $X$, the equation in height coordinates is

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}
=\frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(\rho K_X\frac{\partial X}{\partial z}\right).
$$

From (3.3.9), define

$$
C_\sigma\equiv\frac{g\sigma}{R_dT}
=-\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial z}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=-C_\sigma\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}.
$$

Replacing both the inner and the outer height derivative by σ derivatives gives

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}
=\frac{C_\sigma}{\rho}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}
\left(\rho K_XC_\sigma\frac{\partial X}{\partial\sigma}\right).
$$

Since $\rho=\sigma p_s/(R_dT)$,

$$
\frac{C_\sigma}{\rho}=\frac{g}{p_s},
\qquad
\rho C_\sigma
=\frac{g p_s\sigma^2}{R_d^2T^2}.
$$

$p_s$ is constant along a column, so the two factors combine and the equation becomes

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}
\left(
B_X\frac{\partial X}{\partial\sigma}
\right),
\qquad
B_{X,k+1/2}
=
\left(
\frac{g\sigma_{k+1/2}}{R_dT_{k+1/2}}
\right)^2
K_{X,k+1/2}.
$$

#### Step 2: write the interface gradient as a difference of adjacent layers

Define the distance between adjacent layer centers as

$$
\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}\equiv\sigma_{k+1}-\sigma_k>0.
$$

Here the lowercase $\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}$ is the distance between two level centers, while the
capital $\Delta\sigma_k$ is the thickness of layer $k$ between its two interfaces, as used in the
layer budget below; the two are generally not equal. The interface gradient is approximated by

$$
\left.\frac{\partial X}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{k+1/2}
\approx
\frac{X_{k+1}-X_k}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}},
$$

so the interface quantity is defined as

$$
\mathcal F_{k+1/2}^{(X)}
=B_{X,k+1/2}
\frac{X_{k+1}-X_k}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}.
$$

Integrating $\partial_\sigma\mathcal F$ between the upper and lower boundaries of layer $k$ and
using backward Euler in time gives

$$
\Delta\sigma_k\frac{X_k^+-X_k^-}{\Delta t_*}
=\mathcal F_{k+1/2}^{(X),+}
-\mathcal F_{k-1/2}^{(X),+}.
$$

#### Step 3: combine the time step with the interface coefficient

Multiplying by $\Delta t_*$ directly yields the dimensionless interface coupling coefficient of
the tridiagonal equations:

$$
\boxed{
A_{k+1/2}^{(X)}
=
\frac{\Delta t_*}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}
\left(
\frac{g\sigma_{k+1/2}}{R_dT_{k+1/2}}
\right)^2
K_{X,k+1/2}
}.
$$

The momentum variables $X\in\{u,v\}$ use $K_X=K_m$, and specific humidity uses $K_X=K_h$.
Temperature also uses $K_h$, but because the quantity actually diffused is potential
temperature, its equation carries additional $\sigma^\kappa$ factors. In the code the interface
coefficient is transformed in place after the humidity solve: `zkdiffh` is multiplied by
$\Pi_{h,k+1/2}=\sigma_{h,k+1/2}^{\kappa}$ (`zskaph`) to form the heat coupling

$$
H_{k+1/2}=A_{k+1/2}^{(h)}\,\Pi_{h,k+1/2},
$$

and the layer-to-layer difference is taken between potential temperatures
$\theta_k=T_k/\Pi_k$ with $\Pi_k=\sigma_k^\kappa$ (`zskap`). The two factors sit at different
positions — one on the interface, one on the full level — and must not be canceled.

In the code every interface quantity is stored in an array indexed by the full level immediately
above the interface: element $k$ of `zkdiffh` holds $H_{k+1/2}$, the coupling across the interface
between layers $k$ and $k+1$. This is a storage convention only; in the equations an interface
coupling is always labelled by its half-integer index.

Multiplying by $\Delta t_*$ and replacing the σ gradient by the difference
between adjacent layers gives the exchange across interface $k+1/2$ in one time step,

$$
\Delta t_*\,\mathcal F_{k+1/2}^{(T),+}
=\underbrace{A_{k+1/2}^{(h)}\Pi_{h,k+1/2}}_{H_{k+1/2}}
\left(\theta_{k+1}^+-\theta_k^+\right)
=H_{k+1/2}\left(\frac{T_{k+1}^+}{\Pi_{k+1}}-\frac{T_k^+}{\Pi_k}\right),
$$

The quantity stored in the layer is the temperature $T$, not the potential
temperature, so the left-hand side of the budget of layer $k$ keeps
$\Delta\sigma_k(T_k^+-T_k^-)$ and the two interface fluxes are measured in potential
temperature:

$$
\Delta\sigma_k(T_k^+-T_k^-)
=H_{k-1/2}\left(\frac{T_{k-1}^+}{\Pi_{k-1}}-\frac{T_k^+}{\Pi_k}\right)
+H_{k+1/2}\left(\frac{T_{k+1}^+}{\Pi_{k+1}}-\frac{T_k^+}{\Pi_k}\right).
$$

Thus

$$
-\frac{H_{k-1/2}}{\Pi_{k-1}}T_{k-1}^{+}
+\left[\Delta\sigma_k+\frac{H_{k-1/2}+H_{k+1/2}}{\Pi_k}\right]T_k^{+}
-\frac{H_{k+1/2}}{\Pi_{k+1}}T_{k+1}^{+}
=\Delta\sigma_kT_k^{-}.
$$


#### Step 4: Inserting K-theory

Inserting $K_x=l_x^2S f_x(Ri_g)$ and using the implementation's

$$
S_{k+1/2}
\approx
\frac{g\sigma_{k+1/2}}{R_dT_{k+1/2}}
\frac{\max\!\left(U_{\min},|U_{k+1}-U_k|\right)}
{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}},
$$

the coupling coefficient splits into

$$
A_{k+1/2}^{(x)}
=
\underbrace{
\frac{\Delta t_*}{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}
\left(\frac{g\sigma_{k+1/2}}{R_dT_{k+1/2}}\right)^2
}_{\text{time discretization and coordinate metric}}
\underbrace{l_{x,k+1/2}^2f_x(Ri_{k+1/2})}_{\text{mixing length and stability}}
\underbrace{
\frac{g\sigma_{k+1/2}}{R_dT_{k+1/2}}
\frac{\max\!\left(U_{\min},|U_{k+1}-U_k|\right)}
{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}
}_{\text{height-coordinate shear }S_{k+1/2}}.
$$

$$
\boxed{
A_{k+1/2}^{(x)}
=
\frac{g^3\Delta t_*\,\sigma_{k+1/2}^3}
{R_d^3T_{k+1/2}^3\,\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}
l_{x,k+1/2}^2 f_x(Ri_{k+1/2})
\frac{\max\!\left(U_{\min},|U_{k+1}-U_k|\right)}
{\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}}
},
\qquad x\in\{m,h\}.
$$

Here $x=m$ denotes momentum and $x=h$ denotes heat and moisture. The interface temperature in the
code is the thickness-weighted interpolation of the two adjacent layer temperatures:

$$
T_{k+1/2}
\approx
\frac{
T_k\Delta\sigma_{k+1}
+T_{k+1}\Delta\sigma_k
}{
\Delta\sigma_k+\Delta\sigma_{k+1}
}.
$$

The correspondence between the mathematical quantities and the variables in `vdiff_kernel.c` is:

| Mathematical quantity | Code variable |
|---|---|
| $\Delta t_*$ | `deltsec2` |
| $1/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}$ | `zrdsig` |
| $1/T_{k+1/2}$ | `zrthl` |
| $S_{k+1/2}$ | `(g * sigmah / (R_d * T)) * zdvds` |
| $A^{(m)}_{k+1/2}$ | `zkdiffm` |
| $A^{(h)}_{k+1/2}$ | `zkdiffh` |

`zfac` already combines $\Delta t_*g^3\sigma_{k+1/2}^3/(R_d^3\delta\sigma_{k+1/2})$, while
`zdvds` carries the remaining $1/\delta\sigma_{k+1/2}$ and the wind difference.

### 6.2 The top and bottom layers

Model layers are numbered from the top downward, $k=0,1,\ldots,L$. Hence:

- the top layer is $k=0$, with only the interior interface $1/2$ below it;
- the bottom layer is $k=L$, with only the interior interface $L-1/2$ above it;
- the model-top boundary is denoted $-1/2$ and the model-bottom boundary $L+1/2$.

From above, we konw that:

$$
-A^{(X)}_{k-1/2}X_{k-1}^+
+\left(\Delta\sigma_k+A^{(X)}_{k-1/2}+A^{(X)}_{k+1/2}\right)X_k^+
-A^{(X)}_{k+1/2}X_{k+1}^+
=\Delta\sigma_kX_k^-.
\tag{3.3.19}
$$

An interior layer exchanges with both neighbors, so (3.3.19) contains three new-time unknowns. The
top and bottom layers each lack one neighbor, so their equations each lose one term.

#### Top-layer equation: zero flux at the model top

The complete budget of the top layer is

$$
\Delta\sigma_0(X_0^+-X_0^-)
=\Delta t_*
\left(
\mathcal F_{1/2}^{(X),+}
-\mathcal F_{-1/2}^{(X),+}
\right).
$$

The model top uses the zero-flux boundary condition, $\mathcal F_{-1/2}^{(X),+}=0$. The top layer
can exchange only through the interface $1/2$ with layer 1, so

$$
\Delta\sigma_0(X_0^+-X_0^-)=A^{(X)}_{1/2}(X_1^+-X_0^+),
$$

$$
(\Delta\sigma_0+A^{(X)}_{1/2})X_0^+
-A^{(X)}_{1/2}X_1^+=\Delta\sigma_0X_0^-.
\tag{3.3.20}
$$

#### Bottom-layer equation: zero flux at the model bottom

Similarly, the bottom-layer budget is

$$
-A^{(X)}_{L-1/2}X_{L-1}^+
+(\Delta\sigma_L+A^{(X)}_{L-1/2})X_L^+
=\Delta\sigma_LX_L^-.
\tag{3.3.21}
$$


### 6.3 The Thomas algorithm

In (3.3.19), layer $k$ is coupled only to $k-1$, $k$, and $k+1$. Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_k&=A^{(X)}_{k-1/2},\\
b_k&=\Delta\sigma_k+A^{(X)}_{k-1/2}+A^{(X)}_{k+1/2},\\
c_k&=A^{(X)}_{k+1/2},\\
r_k&=\Delta\sigma_kX_k^-.
\end{aligned}
$$

Then every row has the standard form

$$
-a_kX_{k-1}^+ + b_kX_k^+ - c_kX_{k+1}^+=r_k.
$$

Collecting all layers gives the coefficient matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
b_0 & -c_0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\
-a_1 & b_1 & -c_1 & \ddots & \vdots\\
0 & -a_2 & b_2 & \ddots & 0\\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & -c_{L-1}\\
0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_L & b_L
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
X_0^+\\X_1^+\\X_2^+\\\vdots\\X_L^+
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_0\\r_1\\r_2\\\vdots\\r_L
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The Thomas algorithm does not compute the matrix inverse explicitly; it
exploits this sparse structure by eliminating the subdiagonal from the top downward and then
substituting back from the bottom upward (Golub & Van Loan, 2013).


### 6.4 Why kinetic energy gets its own diffusion solve

When dissipation heating is enabled (`ndheat > 0`, the same switch as in
[3.2](3.2-surface-fluxes.md)), the kernel does not convert the entire kinetic-energy loss of the
momentum solve into heat. Part of that loss is a genuine vertical redistribution of kinetic
energy, so the kernel first applies the same implicit solver, with the same momentum
coefficients, to the kinetic energy per unit mass $K=\tfrac12(u^2+v^2)$. Only the difference
between the actual change and this redistributed change becomes heating:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial T_k}{\partial t}\right|_{\rm fric}
=
-\frac{1}{2c_p(q_k)}
\left[
\frac{\Delta\!\left(u_k^2+v_k^2\right)}{\Delta t_*}
-
\frac{\Delta K_k^{\rm diff}}{\Delta t_*}
\right],
$$

where $\Delta(u_k^2+v_k^2)$ is the change produced by the momentum solve and
$\Delta K_k^{\rm diff}$ is the change obtained by diffusing $K$ alone. If the operator only
redistributed energy, the two changes would be equal and no heating would be produced. Because
the diffused $K$ obeys the same zero-flux boundary conditions as every other scalar, its column
sum is conserved (Section 6.2), so the column-integrated heating equals the total kinetic-energy
loss of the momentum solve (Roeckner et al., 1992). The moist heat capacity $c_p(q)=c_{pd}(1+\alpha_qq)$ uses the local
specific humidity, and the heating is added to the temperature tendency at each level.
